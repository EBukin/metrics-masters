1  Road map

How much do house prices rise when incomes rise? Take the 48 contiguous US states and the District of Columbia, 1975 to 2003, and regress log house prices on log income in each state. The average of the 49 slopes is 0.30: a 10% rise in income goes with a 3% rise in prices. Now allow for one more thing. Every state feels the same national forces, interest rates and credit conditions, each to its own degree. With that allowed for, the elasticity is 1.14. Same data, almost four times the answer (Holly et al. 2010; Croissant and Millo 2019, 197–99). This book is about why.

A panel follows the same units, here states, over several periods. Its promise is that each state is compared with itself over time, so everything fixed about a state drops out. Its catch is that the states are not independent of each other. A national shock moves them all at once, and in the picture below it is hard to see 49 separate stories:

data("HousePricesUS", package = "pder")
lp <- with(HousePricesUS, tapply(log(price), list(year, names), identity))
growth <- diff(lp)
op <- graphics::par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
graphics::matplot(as.numeric(rownames(lp)), lp, type = "l", lty = 1,
                  col = "grey75", xlab = "year", ylab = "log price index",
                  main = "Levels")
graphics::lines(as.numeric(rownames(lp)), rowMeans(lp), lwd = 3)
graphics::matplot(as.numeric(rownames(growth)), growth, type = "l", lty = 1,
                  col = "grey75", xlab = "year", ylab = "annual growth",
                  main = "Growth rates")
graphics::lines(as.numeric(rownames(growth)), rowMeans(growth), lwd = 3)
graphics::par(op)

In equations, state i in year t follows

\[ y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta x_{it} + u_{it}, \qquad u_{it} = \lambda_i f_t + \varepsilon_{it}. \]

Here \(\alpha_i\) is everything fixed about the state, \(f_t\) is the national shock in year t, and \(\lambda_i\) is how strongly state i feels it, its loading on the shock. Fixed effects remove \(\alpha_i\). Year effects remove \(f_t\), but only if every state feels it equally, \(\lambda_i = 1\). When the loadings differ and the shock also moves income, the shock leaks into \(\hat\beta\). That leak is the gap between 0.30 and 1.14.

A panel analysis follows one workflow, not a fixed menu of tests. Build the panel first, since its shape and balance limit every tool that comes after. Then test whether units move together: dependence that is strong and national calls for common factors, dependence that is weak and local calls for a spatial model, in which a neighbour’s outcome or a neighbour’s shock enters a unit’s own equation. Time dependence is a separate layer, asked once the cross-sectional question is settled. Core panel models, fixed and random effects among them, come last, once the error structure is known. The diagram below maps the whole process; each box is a chapter.

The chapters.

  1. Explore the panel. How many units, how many periods, and whether every unit is observed every period decide which tools apply (panel shape).
  2. Cross-sectional dependence. Do units move together more than chance allows? The CD test answers that (CD tests), the exponent α says how strong (α), and when dependence is strong and global, common correlated effects removes it (CCE). This chapter gives the 1.14 above.
  3. Spatial dependence. When dependence is weak and local, neighbours matter more than the whole panel. Write the map as a weights matrix (weights), test whether the dependence follows it (does it follow the map), choose between a neighbour’s outcome and a neighbour’s shock (lag or error), fit it (fixed effects), and read the coefficients as effects on the map (direct and indirect effects).
  4. Time dependence. Do series wander with no level to return to (why it matters)? First-generation tests assume independent units (first generation), second-generation tests allow a common factor (second generation), and the fraction of series that reject the null needs its own test (fraction), past a set of traps (traps, summary). When two series wander together, the question is whether they share a long run (cointegration, the long run).
  5. Core panel models. Can one regression serve every unit (poolability), is there a unit effect at all (effects tests), should it be fixed or random (fixed or random), does the error remember last year (serial correlation), and which standard errors survive the dependence found earlier (robust errors).
  6. Dynamic panels. With last year’s outcome among the regressors, fixed effects is biased (the bias); instruments built from earlier lags repair it (difference and system GMM), and the diagnostics say whether they did (diagnostics).
