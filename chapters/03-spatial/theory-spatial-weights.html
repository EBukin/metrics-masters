Appendix C — Spatial weights

What W assumes before it estimates anything

Code: spatial weights. Related notes: testing for spatial dependence, spatial model choice and cross-sectional dependence.

C.1 Why there has to be a matrix

Time has an order and a direction: 1999 comes before 2000, and the past can move the future but not the reverse. A cross-section of states has neither. Arizona is not “before” Nevada, and if Nevada’s prices move Arizona’s, Arizona’s move Nevada’s in the same year. To say anything about dependence across units, the analyst has to supply the order that time supplies for free: a statement of which units are close to which, and how close. That statement is the spatial weights matrix W, N × N, with \(w_{ij}\) the weight of unit j in unit i’s row and zeros on the diagonal.

Nothing in the data produces W. It is chosen, and Anselin (1988, 19) calls that choice “one of the most controversial subjects in spatial econometrics”. Every estimate in this chapter is an estimate given W.

C.2 1. Three ways to say neighbour

Contiguity. \(w_{ij} = 1\) when i and j share a border, 0 otherwise; “queen” contiguity counts a shared corner as a border, “rook” does not. This is usaw49 for the states and riceww for the farms, where the border is “same village”. Binary contiguity imposes the least structure, makes \(Wy\) the plain average of the neighbours, and is the usual choice when the aim is to test for spatial effects rather than to estimate a theoretically given process (Anselin 1988, 21; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 10.2).

Nearest neighbours. \(w_{ij} = 1\) for the k units closest to i. Every unit gets exactly k neighbours, which keeps W balanced where units differ wildly in size, at the price of links that cross no border, as the long lines in the West of the weights section show.

Distance. \(w_{ij} = 1\), or \(1/d_{ij}\), for every j within a cutoff of i, so that influence fades with distance and the cutoff says where it stops. Dense where units are small, thin where they are large.

Whichever is chosen, the chapter assumes that W is exogenous and fixed over time. Geography is exogenous; a matrix of bilateral trade shares, built from the same economy as the outcome, is not (Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 10.2). The distance need not be geographic: a space of economic or demographic characteristics works the same way, as long as it is not an outcome of the model.

C.3 2. Row standardisation and what ρ means

Dividing each row by its sum makes \(\sum_j w_{ij} = 1\), and then

\[ (Wy)_i = \sum_j w_{ij}\, y_j \]

is the average outcome of i’s neighbours. The spatial parameter ρ then reads as a share: ρ = 0.65 on the defactored house-price residuals means a state’s residual carries 0.65 of its neighbours’ average residual. Two costs. W stops being symmetric: Maine has one neighbour and gives it weight 1; New Hampshire has three and gives Maine 1/3. And ρ stops being comparable across matrices: with four nearest neighbours every weight is 0.25, with a distance band the average weight is whatever the density makes it, and the same data return a different ρ̂ under each. The chapter reports every ρ̂ as a number for usaw49.

C.4 3. The parameter space

A spatial lag or error model has to invert \(I - \rho W\). The inverse exists whenever ρ is not the reciprocal of an eigenvalue of W, so the admissible interval around zero runs from \(1/\omega_{\min}\) to \(1/\omega_{\max}\), with ω the real eigenvalues. For a row-standardised W, \(\omega_{\max} = 1\), so the upper bound is 1; the lower bound is whatever the most negative eigenvalue gives, −1.39 for usaw49.

A bound that needs no eigenvalues is (Kelejian and Prucha 2010; Pesaran 2015, 799)

\[ |\rho| < \frac{1}{\tau^*}, \qquad \tau^* = \min\big(\lVert W\rVert_1, \lVert W\rVert_\infty\big), \]

the smaller of the largest column sum and the largest row sum. With rows standardised, \(\lVert W\rVert_\infty = 1\) and so \(\tau^* \le 1\): any \(|\rho| < 1\) is safe. The familiar statement “ρ lies in (−1, 1)” is exact only when W is row and column standardised (Pesaran 2015, 802), and usaw49’s column sums run from 0.33 to 1.7.

The eigenvalues matter for a second reason. The likelihood of every model in this chapter carries \(\ln|I - \rho W| = \sum_i \ln(1 - \rho\,\omega_i)\), and spml’s default method = "eigen" computes exactly that sum at every step of the optimiser. For N in the thousands the eigenvalues are too expensive, and the sparse-matrix and Chebyshev approximations take over (Millo and Piras 2012, sec. 5.2).

C.5 4. Granularity: why a lag is endogenous

A spatial lag \(Wy\) is endogenous, and a within fit that includes it is biased, because \(y_j\) for a neighbour j contains \(u_j\), which is correlated with \(u_i\) through the same W. Pesaran (2015, 800) make the condition precise: the covariance of \((Wy)_{it}\) with \(u_{it}\) stays away from zero only when the weights are non-granular, that is, when some \(w_{ij}\) stay bounded away from zero as N grows. If every weight were of order \(1/N\), the lag would be a cross-section average, its covariance with any one unit’s error would vanish, and there would be nothing to instrument.

This is the bridge to the previous chapter. A cross-section average is a spatial lag with a granular W, every weight \(1/N\); it proxies a factor that every unit loads on, and CCE puts it on the right-hand side as an exogenous control. A contiguity W, with a handful of weights near 1/4 in every row, is the opposite case: its lag is endogenous, the dependence it generates is weak, and α stays below 1 (Pesaran 2015, sec. 30.3.3). The largest weight in usaw49 is 1, Maine’s.

C.6 5. Sparsity: what the model links

W is sparse: 9% of usaw49 is non-zero. The covariance of a spatial error model is not. With \(u = (I - \lambda W)^{-1}\varepsilon\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}(u) = \sigma^2 \big[(I - \lambda W)'(I - \lambda W)\big]^{-1}, \]

and the inverse of a sparse matrix is dense: 99.7% of the cells of \((I - 0.5W)^{-1}\) are non-zero. A shock in California reaches Maine, in any spatial autoregressive model, through the chain of neighbours in between (Pesaran 2015, 801). Two alternative error forms keep the covariance as sparse as W: the spatial moving average, \(u = \varepsilon + \lambda W\varepsilon\), and the spatial error components model, \(u = W\xi + \varepsilon\) (Pesaran 2015, eqs. 30.5–30.6). They say a shock stops at the first neighbour. Neither is implemented in splm, whose spatial.error is the autoregressive form only, so the sharpest distinction in the theory is not available to the code.

C.7 If / then

If Then
The question is whether space matters at all Binary contiguity, row-standardised (Anselin 1988, 21)
Units differ wildly in size k nearest neighbours; say what k and why
A ρ̂ is reported above 1 The fit is broken, or W is not row-standardised; check \(\lVert W\rVert_\infty\)
Two papers report ρ̂ on different W They are not comparable; compare the impacts instead (effects)
W is built from an outcome of the model (trade, migration) It is endogenous; the chapter’s estimators do not apply
Every weight is of order 1/N The lag is a cross-section average, and the question is a factor, not space
A shock should stop at the first neighbour The autoregressive error is the wrong form, and the right one is not implemented: say so
