Appendix J — Fixed and random effects

The unit effect, the tests that find it, and the test between them

Code: effects tests and fixed or random. The poolability and slope heterogeneity steps send readers to section 1 here and to the error of a panel regression for the tests. Related notes: common factors and CCE and fitting spatial panels.

J.1 Why the method exists

General Motors invests on a scale Westinghouse never reaches, at the same market value and the same capital stock. Each of Grunfeld’s ten firms (Grunfeld 1958) has a character of its own, and so does each of the 49 states whose house prices run through this book. Call that character \(\alpha_i\), the unit effect: everything about unit i that does not change over the sample and is not in the regression. In an earnings equation it is unobserved ability; in a production function it is managerial skill (Baltagi 2021, 15).

One pooled regression puts \(\alpha_i\) in the error, and that does two kinds of damage. The errors of one unit are then correlated across every pair of years by the same amount, so the pooled standard errors are wrong (Moulton 1986; Baltagi 2021, 24, 54). And if \(\alpha_i\) moves with the regressors, as ability moves with schooling, the pooled slope is biased: an omitted-variable bias, since the unit dummies that belong in the equation were left out (Baltagi 2021, 18).

Fixed effects treat \(\alpha_i\) as a parameter, one per unit, and either estimate it with a dummy or wipe it out by taking each unit’s data in deviation from its own mean. The price is that only within-unit variation is left: \(N - 1\) degrees of freedom are gone, and any regressor that does not change over time, sex, schooling, a state’s land area, is wiped out with the effect (Baltagi 2021, 17). Random effects treat \(\alpha_i\) as a draw, \(\alpha_i \sim \mathrm{IID}(0, \sigma^2_\alpha)\), independent of the regressors. The price is that independence assumption; the gain is efficiency, because between-unit variation is used too, and a coefficient on the time-invariant regressors (Baltagi 2021, 17, 24, 27). Mundlak put the choice sharply: random effects assume every regressor is uncorrelated with \(\alpha_i\), fixed effects allow every one to be correlated, “an ‘all’ or ‘nothing’ choice” (Mundlak 1978; Baltagi 2021, 28). The rest of this note is about how to tell whether \(\alpha_i\) is there at all, and then which of the two stories fits.

J.2 1. One error, two components

One regression for all units assumes two things: the same slopes for every unit, and an error whose only structure is the unit effect. Write the model as

\[ y_{it} = \beta' x_{it} + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad u_{it} = \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it}, \]

with \(\varepsilon_{it} \sim \mathrm{IID}(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\), the one-way error component model (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 2.1–2.2, p. 15). The two-way model adds a time effect, \(u_{it} = \alpha_i + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{it}\), a strike year or an oil shock that hits every unit in that year (Baltagi 2021, eq. 3.1, p. 47); Baltagi writes it \(\lambda_t\). The same-slopes assumption is poolability, and its tests, Chow’s F, Roy–Zellner and Swamy’s random coefficients (Chow 1960; Roy 1957; Zellner 1962; Swamy 1970), are in the error note; this note takes the slopes as common and asks what \(\alpha_i\) is.

Under random effects the composite error has covariance

\[ \Omega = \sigma^2_\alpha (I_N \otimes J_T) + \sigma^2_\varepsilon I_{NT}, \]

where \(J_T\) is a \(T \times T\) matrix of ones (Baltagi 2021, eq. 2.17, p. 24). Two errors of the same unit, any two years apart, are correlated by \(\rho = \sigma^2_\alpha / (\sigma^2_\alpha + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\), however far apart the years; errors of different units are uncorrelated (Baltagi 2021, 24). That constant \(\rho\) is the signature of a unit effect, and the serial-correlation tests of the error note are built to tell it from an autoregressive error, whose correlation dies out with the distance between years.

J.3 2. Within, between and GLS

Two projection matrices do all the work. \(P = I_N \otimes \bar J_T\), with \(\bar J_T = J_T / T\), replaces every observation by its unit mean; \(Q = I_{NT} - P\) takes deviations from the unit mean. They are symmetric, idempotent, orthogonal, and sum to the identity (Baltagi 2021, 16; Croissant and Millo 2019, 25). The within estimator regresses \(Qy\) on \(QX\),

\[ \hat\beta_W = (X'QX)^{-1} X'Qy, \]

and \(Q\) wipes out \(\alpha_i\) with the unit means (Baltagi 2021, eq. 2.7, p. 17). The between estimator regresses the unit means on each other, \(\hat\beta_B = (X'PX)^{-1} X'Py\), and uses only the variation across units (Baltagi 2021, eq. 2.25, p. 26). Pooled OLS weights the two sources of variation equally; GLS weights them by the inverse of their variances (Baltagi 2021, 26–27). With \(\phi^2 = \sigma^2_\varepsilon / \sigma^2_1\) and \(\sigma^2_1 = T\sigma^2_\alpha + \sigma^2_\varepsilon\),

\[ \hat\beta_{GLS} = W_1 \hat\beta_W + W_2 \hat\beta_B, \qquad W_1 = [X'QX + \phi^2 X'PX]^{-1} X'QX, \quad W_2 = I - W_1, \]

a matrix-weighted average of within and between (Baltagi 2021, eq. 2.31, pp. 26–27; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 2.4.1, p. 39). Two limits say what the estimator is doing. If \(\sigma^2_\alpha = 0\) then \(\phi^2 = 1\) and GLS is OLS. If \(T \to \infty\) then \(\phi^2 \to 0\) and GLS is within (Baltagi 2021, 27; Pesaran 2015, 653): with enough years per unit the two stories give the same slope, and the fixed-or-random question only bites when T is short.

In practice GLS is OLS on quasi-demeaned data. Premultiplying by \(\Omega^{-1/2}\) turns \(y_{it}\) into \(y_{it} - \theta \bar y_{i\cdot}\) with

\[ \theta = 1 - \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon}{\sigma_1} = 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + T\sigma^2_\alpha / \sigma^2_\varepsilon}}, \]

the fraction of the unit mean that is removed (Baltagi 2021, eq. 2.20, p. 25; Croissant and Millo 2019, 35). \(\theta = 0\) is pooled OLS, \(\theta = 1\) is within, and plm::ercomp prints it: 0.86 on Grunfeld, which is why every feasible GLS fit there sits next to the within fit (Baltagi 2021, 31).

The variance components have to be estimated first, and the menu in random.method is a menu of residuals. Wallace–Hussain use pooled OLS residuals in the two quadratic forms \(u'Pu/N\) and \(u'Qu/N(T-1)\) (Wallace and Hussain 1969); Amemiya uses the within residuals instead, which gives the same asymptotic distribution as knowing the true disturbances (Amemiya 1971; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 2.21–2.23, p. 25); Swamy–Arora take \(\hat\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) from the within regression’s mean square and \(\hat\sigma^2_1\) from the between regression’s (Swamy and Arora 1972; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 2.24 and 2.27, pp. 25–26); Nerlove estimates \(\sigma^2_\alpha\) as the sample variance of the estimated dummies (Nerlove 1971; Baltagi 2021, 27; Croissant and Millo 2019, 36–37). All but Nerlove recover \(\sigma^2_\alpha = (\sigma^2_1 - \sigma^2_\varepsilon)/T\) by subtraction, and nothing stops the difference from being negative. In the one-way model the fix is to set it to zero, which happens only when the true variance is near zero, when OLS is fine anyway, so “replacing negative \(\sigma^2_\mu\) by zero is not a sin after all” (Baltagi 2021, 27). In the two-way model Baltagi’s Monte Carlo says the opposite: a negative estimate cannot be told from a misspecified model, and “the researcher should not label the problem of negative variance estimates ‘not serious’” (Baltagi 1981; 2021, 56).

The two-way model has four distinct roots of \(\Omega\) and three variance components, so GLS is a matrix-weighted average of three estimators: within, between units \(\hat\beta_B\) and between periods \(\hat\beta_C\) (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 3.13 and 3.24, pp. 51–53), and Swamy–Arora’s recipe needs three regressions (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 3.19–3.21, p. 52). The time variance component is estimated from \(T - 1\) period means, so on a panel with 20 years it is the one most likely to come back negative. That is what happens to Grunfeld under Swamy–Arora and Wallace–Hussain (Baltagi 2021, 99), and it is why the two-way Hausman statistic of section 5 depends on the method.

J.4 3. Is there an effect at all

The Breusch–Pagan test asks whether pooled OLS residuals from one unit resemble each other more than chance allows. It is a Lagrange multiplier test: a score test that needs only the restricted model, here pooled OLS, and under normality the score for \(\sigma^2_\alpha\) is a function of the ratio of the summed squared unit totals to the summed squared residuals (Breusch and Pagan 1980; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.17–4.22, pp. 81–82). For \(H_0: \sigma^2_\alpha = 0\),

\[ LM_1 = \frac{NT}{2(T-1)} \left[ \frac{\hat u'(I_N \otimes J_T)\hat u}{\hat u'\hat u} - 1 \right]^2 \;\sim\; \chi^2_1 , \]

and the test of \(\sigma^2_\delta = 0\) swaps \(I_N \otimes J_T\) for \(J_N \otimes I_T\) and \(T - 1\) for \(N - 1\); the two-way test of both is their sum, \(\chi^2_2\) (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.22–4.24, p. 82).

The alternative of a two-sided test is “the variance is not zero”, and a variance cannot be negative. Honda takes the signed square root,

\[ A = \sqrt{\frac{NT}{2(T-1)}} \left[ \frac{\hat u'(I_N \otimes J_T)\hat u}{\hat u'\hat u} - 1 \right] \;\sim\; N(0, 1), \]

and rejects only for large positive values; it is uniformly most powerful for the one-sided alternative and robust to non-normality (Honda 1985; Baltagi 2021, eq. 4.25, p. 83). The sign matters. On Grunfeld the time-effect statistic is \(B = -2.540\): the one-sided test does not reject, but \(B^2 = 6.454\) is above the \(\chi^2_1\) critical value 3.841 and the two-sided test does. The one-sided test is right; “these data clearly support the use of the one-sided test in empirical applications” (Baltagi 2021, 87).

For both effects at once Honda offers the “handy” \((A + B)/\sqrt 2\), which is \(N(0, 1)\) under the null (Baltagi 2021, 83). King–Wu replace the equal weights by the locally mean most powerful ones,

\[ KW = \sqrt{\frac{T-1}{N+T-2}}\, A + \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N+T-2}}\, B , \]

also \(N(0, 1)\) (King and Wu 1997; Baltagi et al. 1992; Baltagi 2021, eq. 4.30, p. 84). Both can be dragged down by a negative piece. Gourieroux, Holly and Monfort drop a negative piece instead of adding it:

\[ \chi^2_m = \begin{cases} A^2 + B^2 & A > 0,\ B > 0 \\ A^2 & A > 0,\ B \le 0 \\ B^2 & A \le 0,\ B > 0 \\ 0 & A \le 0,\ B \le 0 , \end{cases} \qquad \chi^2_m \sim \tfrac14 \chi^2(0) + \tfrac12 \chi^2(1) + \tfrac14 \chi^2(2), \]

a mixed chi-square whose weights come from A and B being asymptotically independent; it “is immune to the possible negative values of A and B” (Gourieroux et al. 1982; Baltagi et al. 1992; Baltagi 2021, eq. 4.33, p. 84). In Baltagi, Chang and Li’s Monte Carlo, Breusch–Pagan, Honda and King–Wu all under-size the joint test when a piece is negative, while GHM and the F test hold their size (Baltagi et al. 1992; Baltagi 2021, 86). plm::plmtest(type = "ghm") exists for effect = "twoways" only, which is where it is needed.

Two refinements are not implemented. Moulton and Randolph showed that the \(N(0, 1)\) approximation for Honda’s statistic “can be poor even in large samples … when the number of regressors is large or the intra-class correlation of some of the regressors is high”, and proposed the standardized LM test, Honda’s statistic centred and scaled by the exact mean and variance of the quadratic form, \(SLM = (d - E(d))/\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(d)}\) with \(d = \hat u' D \hat u / \hat u'\hat u\) (Moulton and Randolph 1989; Honda 1991; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.26–4.28 and 4.31, pp. 83–84). Spatially smooth regressors have a high intra-class correlation, so this is the case of this book. And testing \(\sigma^2_\alpha = 0\) with Honda’s statistic “implicitly assumes that the time-specific effects do not exist”, which “may lead to incorrect decisions especially when the variance of the time effects (assumed to be zero) is large”. The conditional LM tests of Baltagi, Chang and Li test one variance allowing the other to be positive, from one-way GLS residuals (Baltagi et al. 1992; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.34 and 4.36, p. 85); the Monte Carlo verdict is that “one should not ignore the possibility that \(\sigma^2_\lambda > 0\) when testing \(\sigma^2_\mu = 0\)”, and that over-specifying the model as two-way costs little (Baltagi 2021, 86). plmtest(effect = "twoways") tests both variances jointly; it is not this conditional test.

The oldest test is an F on the unit dummies: pooled OLS is the restricted model, within the unrestricted, and

\[ F = \frac{(RRSS - URSS)/(N-1)}{URSS/(NT - N - K)} \;\sim\; F_{N-1,\, N(T-1)-K} \]

[Baltagi (2021), eq. 2.12, p. 18; eq. 4.38, p. 85]. In the two-way model there are three such tests: both sets of dummies against pooled OLS, unit dummies given time dummies, and time dummies given unit dummies, each with the two-way within fit as the unrestricted model (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 3.8–3.9, p. 49). These conditional F tests are exact in finite samples under normality (Baltagi 2021, 85), “perform reasonably well when compared to the LR and LM tests, for both the one-way and two-way models and are recommended” (Baltagi 2021, 87); they are the three rows of Baltagi’s Table 4.3 (Baltagi 2021, 89), and plm::pFtest on two nested within fits is the conditional pair.

J.5 4. Fixed or random: the Hausman contrast

Everything above finds an effect; nothing says whether it is correlated with the regressors. The random-effects GLS estimator is efficient when \(E(u_{it} \mid X_{it}) = 0\) and inconsistent otherwise; the within estimator is consistent either way, because \(Q\) removed \(\alpha_i\) before the regression. Hausman’s idea is to compare them: if both are consistent they should agree, and the covariance of an efficient estimator with its difference from any other consistent estimator is zero, so the variance of the difference is the difference of the variances (Hausman 1978; Baltagi 2021, 89–90; Pesaran 2015, eq. 26.52, p. 660). With \(\hat q_1 = \hat\beta_{GLS} - \hat\beta_W\),

\[ \mathrm{var}(\hat q_1) = \mathrm{var}(\hat\beta_W) - \mathrm{var}(\hat\beta_{GLS}), \qquad m_1 = \hat q_1' [\mathrm{var}(\hat q_1)]^{-1} \hat q_1 \;\sim\; \chi^2_K \]

(Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.40–4.41, p. 90). The same test is a Wald test in an augmented regression: quasi-demean \(y\) and \(X\) as GLS does, add the within-demeaned regressors \(\tilde X = QX\), and test their coefficient,

\[ y^* = X^*\beta + \tilde X \gamma + w, \qquad H_0: \gamma = 0 \]

(Baltagi 2021, eq. 4.42, p. 90). OLS on this regression returns \(\hat\beta = \hat\beta_B\) and \(\hat\gamma = \hat\beta_W - \hat\beta_B\), with \(\mathrm{var}(\hat\gamma) = \mathrm{var}(\hat\beta_W) + \mathrm{var}(\hat\beta_B)\), a sum, since the two use orthogonal pieces of the data (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.46–4.48, pp. 90–91). Hausman and Taylor proved that the three contrasts, GLS against within, GLS against between, and within against between, give numerically identical statistics (Hausman and Taylor 1981; Baltagi 2021, 91); phtest(method = "chisq") is \(m_1\) and method = "aux" is the augmented regression. On Grunfeld they are 2.33 and 2.13, the difference being only which \(\hat\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) enters the variance (Baltagi 2021, 95).

The augmented regression is also how the test is made robust. Under heteroskedasticity or serial correlation neither estimator is efficient, so the variance of the contrast is no longer a difference of variances and \(m_1\) is wrong. Arellano writes the contrast as a regression on forward orthogonal deviations stacked over unit means, with \(\hat\gamma = \hat\beta_B - \hat\beta_W\), and tests \(\gamma = 0\) with White’s robust covariance (Arellano 1993; Baltagi 2021, eq. 4.49, pp. 91–92); “a simpler robust Hausman test can be obtained by testing for \(\gamma = 0\) from the artificial regression (4.42) using the robust variance-covariance matrix option” (Baltagi 2021, 92; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 5.1.3.1, p. 125), which is phtest(method = "aux", vcov = plm::vcovHC). On Grunfeld Baltagi’s robust F(2, 195) is 1.58, p 0.208 (Baltagi 2021, 95); with ten clusters the Arellano covariance is itself noisy, since the estimator is asymptotic in the number of clusters (Croissant and Millo 2019, 110), and the step reports what plm gives.

Mundlak’s reading says what is being tested. Let the unit effect be a linear function of the unit means of the regressors,

\[ \alpha_i = \pi' \bar x_{i\cdot} + w_i, \qquad w_i \sim \mathrm{IID}(0, \sigma^2_w). \]

Substituting it in gives \(y = X\beta + PX\pi + (w + \varepsilon)\), with \(w\) the \(w_i\) stacked over the \(NT\) observations, an error-components model with the unit means as extra regressors. GLS on it returns \(\hat\beta = \hat\beta_W\) and \(\hat\pi = \hat\beta_B - \hat\beta_W\): once the correlation is modelled, the efficient estimator is within, and the random-effects estimator is biased because it ignores \(\pi\) (Mundlak 1978; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 7.33–7.38, pp. 170–171; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 4.2.1, pp. 89–90). Hausman’s test is the test of \(\pi = 0\). It is worth saying what a rejection does not show. “Rejecting the null of the Hausman test implies that the RE estimator is not consistent. This does not necessarily mean that the FE restrictions are satisfied” (Baltagi 2021, 93). Chamberlain’s restrictions, that the reduced-form regression of each year’s \(y\) on every year’s \(x\) has the structure \(\Pi = (I_T \otimes \beta') + \iota_T \lambda'\), are testable; Angrist and Newey showed that the minimum chi-square statistic is a sum over periods of degrees of freedom times the \(R^2\) from regressing that period’s within residuals on all leads and lags of the regressors (Chamberlain 1982; Angrist and Newey 1991; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.50–4.53, pp. 92–93; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 4.2.3, pp. 90–93). Both need N large against \(TK\): the statistic has \((T^2 - T - 1)K\) degrees of freedom (Croissant and Millo 2019, 93), and the minimum chi-square test understates its variance as T grows (Baltagi 2021, 93). plm::piest fails on ten firms and on the 595 workers; aneweytest runs on the workers, and both run on the 171 rice farms. One more caution: using the Hausman test to pick the estimator and then testing \(\beta\) distorts the size of the second test, and Guggenberger’s advice is fixed-effects inference without the pretest (Guggenberger 2010; Baltagi 2021, 94).

J.6 5. The two-way case

With a time effect as well, the contrast of two-way GLS with two-way within is still a Hausman test, and the augmented regression still works with the two-way transformations (Baltagi 2021, 99). What breaks is the equivalence of section 4. There are now two between estimators, across units \(\hat\beta_C\) and across periods \(\hat\beta_T\), and GLS is a weighted combination of three; Kang showed that the test based on within minus GLS is not equivalent to the one based on either between estimator minus GLS, and listed five testable hypotheses (Kang 1985; Baltagi 2021, 99):

  1. \(\alpha_i\) fixed, test \(E(\delta_t \mid X) = 0\) from \(\hat\beta_W - \hat\beta_T\);
  2. \(\alpha_i\) random, test the same from \(\hat\beta_T - \hat\beta_{GLS}\);
  3. \(\delta_t\) fixed, test \(E(\alpha_i \mid X) = 0\) from \(\hat\beta_W - \hat\beta_C\);
  4. \(\delta_t\) random, test the same from \(\hat\beta_C - \hat\beta_{GLS}\);
  5. both fixed against both random, from \(\hat\beta_{GLS} - \hat\beta_W\).

plm::phtest(effect = "twoways") is hypothesis 5 and nothing else. On Grunfeld it rejects: Baltagi’s 8.842 on \(\chi^2_2\), p 0.012 (Baltagi 2021, 99–100), computed with EViews’ Wansbeek–Kapteyn variance components because Swamy–Arora and Wallace–Hussain return a negative \(\hat\sigma^2_\delta\) there (Wansbeek and Kapteyn 1989; Baltagi 2021, 99). plm has no Wansbeek–Kapteyn option and truncates the negative component to zero, so the statistic it prints depends on random.method, and none of its values is 8.842. The reading does not change: every diagnostic of section 3 says there is no time effect on Grunfeld, so “one should not employ a two-way random effects model, but rather a one-way random individual effects model” (Baltagi 2021, 101).

J.7 6. Time-invariant regressors

A rejection sends the reader to fixed effects, and fixed effects cannot estimate the return to schooling, because schooling does not change over the seven years of the wage panel. Hausman and Taylor keep some of the random-effects story. Split the regressors into \(X = [X_1, X_2]\) and the time-invariant ones into \(Z = [Z_1, Z_2]\), where the “1” blocks are uncorrelated with \(\alpha_i\) and the “2” blocks are correlated with it but not with \(\varepsilon_{it}\):

\[ y_{it} = \beta' x_{it} + \gamma' z_i + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it} \]

(Hausman and Taylor 1981; Baltagi 2021, eq. 7.40, p. 171; Pesaran 2015, sec. 26.10, p. 665). The within transformation sweeps \(\alpha_i\) and \(z_i\) together. The HT estimator is instead 2SLS on the \(\Omega^{-1/2}\)-transformed model with instruments the panel generates itself, \(A_{HT} = [QX_1, QX_2, PX_1, Z_1]\): deviations of every time-varying regressor, which are orthogonal to \(\alpha_i\) by construction, plus the unit means of the exogenous ones and the exogenous time-invariant regressors (Baltagi 2021, 171; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 6.3.2.3, p. 146). \(X_1\) is used twice, as means and as deviations, so the order condition is \(k_1 \ge g_2\): at least as many exogenous time-varying regressors as endogenous time-invariant ones (Baltagi 2021, 171; Croissant and Millo 2019, 146). If \(k_1 = g_2\) the slopes on \(X\) are the within slopes; if \(k_1 > g_2\) the model is over-identified, HT is more efficient than within, and a Hausman contrast of the two tests the over-identifying restrictions, \(\chi^2\) with \(k_1 - g_2\) degrees of freedom (Baltagi 2021, eq. 7.46, p. 172). On the wage panel Baltagi reads \(\chi^2_3 = 5.26\) as not rejecting, with three degrees of freedom “since there are three over-identifying conditions” (Baltagi 2021, 175); plm::phtest reports the number of common coefficients instead, nine, and the step says so.

Amemiya and MaCurdy add \(X_1\) at every period as instruments, which needs \(X_1\) to be uncorrelated with \(\alpha_i\) period by period and not only on average (Amemiya and MaCurdy 1986; Baltagi 2021, 173; Croissant and Millo 2019, 147); Breusch, Mizon and Schmidt add the deviations of \(X_2\) at every period too (Breusch et al. 1989; Baltagi 2021, 173). Each set nests the one before, so the extra assumptions are testable by a Hausman contrast of the two fits (Croissant and Millo 2019, 147), and the Cornwell–Rupert replication is the book’s worked case: GLS puts the return to schooling at 10 %, HT at 13.8 %, and the HT-versus-AM contrast does not reject (Cornwell and Rupert 1988; Baltagi and Khanti-Akom 1990; Baltagi 2021, 174–76). When \(z_i\) is uncorrelated with \(\alpha_i\) no instrument is needed: regress the unit means of the within residuals on \(z_i\) (Pesaran 2015, sec. 26.10.1, pp. 663–664).

J.8 If / then

If Then
Honda’s statistic is negative No effect of that kind; do not read the squared Breusch–Pagan value as a rejection (Baltagi 2021, 87)
Both effects are in question Read GHM or the conditional F tests, which hold their size when a piece is negative (Baltagi 2021, 86–87)
Regressors are many or spatially smooth Honda’s \(N(0, 1)\) reference can be poor; the SLM correction is not implemented (Baltagi 2021, 83)
An effect is found and Hausman does not reject Random effects is admissible and more efficient; report it beside within (Baltagi 2021, 90)
Hausman rejects Random effects is inconsistent; fixed effects is not thereby validated, and the Chamberlain restrictions remain to be tested (Baltagi 2021, 93)
Errors are heteroskedastic or serially correlated The \(\chi^2\) form is wrong; use the augmented regression with a robust covariance (Baltagi 2021, 92)
T is long GLS and within converge; the choice matters little (Baltagi 2021, 27; Pesaran 2015, 653)
The two-way time variance comes back negative The two-way Hausman statistic depends on the variance method; check whether a time effect is there at all (Baltagi 2021, 56, 99–101)
A time-invariant regressor matters and is endogenous Hausman–Taylor with \(k_1 \ge g_2\); test the instruments against within (Baltagi 2021, 171–72)

J.9 Open

  • The standardized LM tests of Moulton–Randolph and Honda, Baltagi’s eq. 4.26, are not implemented: plmtest has no type = "slm", and the “Standardized” rows of Baltagi’s Table 4.2 cannot be reproduced (Baltagi 2021, 83, 88).
  • The conditional LM tests of Baltagi, Chang and Li, eqs. 4.34 and 4.36, are not implemented; plmtest(effect = "twoways") is the joint test (Baltagi 2021, 85).
  • Kang’s five hypotheses have one implementation, hypothesis 5; phtest(effect = "time") compares one-way time fixed with one-way time random effects and is none of Baltagi’s Table 4.6 (Baltagi 2021, 99–100).
  • Wansbeek–Kapteyn variance components are not a random.method, so Baltagi’s two-way 8.842 is not reproduced (Baltagi 2021, 99).
  • Chamberlain’s and Angrist–Newey’s tests need \(N \gg TK\), with \((T^2 - T - 1)K\) restrictions to test (Croissant and Millo 2019, 93; Baltagi 2021, 93); piest fails on Grunfeld and on the wage panel, and the chapter reproduces both tests on the rice farms.

J.10 Next

The unit effect is one kind of error structure. The others, unequal variances and memory in \(\varepsilon_{it}\), and the standard errors that survive them, are in the error of a panel regression.
