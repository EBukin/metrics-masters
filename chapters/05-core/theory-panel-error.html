Appendix K — The error of a panel regression

Same slopes, same variance, no memory: tests, repairs, robust errors

Code: poolability, slope heterogeneity, serial correlation, heteroskedasticity and robust errors. Related notes: fixed and random effects and fitting spatial panels.

K.1 Why one regression can lie

One investment equation for ten firms, one price–income elasticity for 49 states: a pooled regression makes three promises about its error. The slopes are the same for every unit, so that what is left over is noise and not a different line. The noise has the same variance everywhere, so that General Motors’ shocks are no wilder than Diamond Match’s. And the noise has no memory, so that this year’s shock says nothing about next year’s beyond what the unit effect already says. Each promise can be broken, each break has a test, and each test has a repair.

The repairs are not the same kind. A wrong slope is a bias, and only a different estimator fixes it. Unequal variances and memory leave the slopes consistent and make the standard errors wrong, in either direction (Baltagi 2021, 109, 115), so the first repair is an honest standard error, and the second, when the structure is known well enough, is an estimator that reweights the data and recovers efficiency. This note takes the three promises in turn and ends with the menu of standard errors.

K.2 1. The composite error and its memory

Write the error of a one-way panel regression as \(u_{it} = \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it}\), where \(\alpha_i\) is the unit effect, everything fixed about unit i that the regression leaves out, and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is the remainder, the shock of unit i in year t (fixed and random effects). Under random effects the covariance is \(\Omega = \sigma^2_\alpha (I_N \otimes J_T) + \sigma^2_\varepsilon I_{NT}\), so two errors of the same unit are correlated by \(\sigma^2_\alpha / (\sigma^2_\alpha + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\) whatever the gap between the years (Baltagi 2021, eq. 2.17, p. 24). A unit effect is therefore “serial correlation that does not die out over time”, and any test for serial correlation run on pooled residuals will find it (Croissant and Millo 2019, 95).

Memory of the other kind decays. Let the remainder follow a first-order autoregression, an AR(1),

\[ \varepsilon_{it} = \rho\, \varepsilon_{i,t-1} + \eta_{it}, \qquad |\rho| < 1, \quad \eta_{it} \sim \mathrm{IID}(0, \sigma^2_\eta), \]

so that a shock to investment this year carries into the next few years and fades (Baltagi 2021, eq. 5.8, p. 115). The covariance of a unit’s errors is then

\[ E(u_i u_i') = \sigma^2_\alpha J_T + \sigma^2_\varepsilon V_\rho, \qquad V_\rho = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho & \cdots & \rho^{T-1} \\ \rho & 1 & \cdots & \rho^{T-2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \rho^{T-1} & \rho^{T-2} & \cdots & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

a constant floor from \(\alpha_i\) plus a band that thins with the distance between years: the AR(1) counterpart, on p. 126, of Baltagi’s eq. 5.32, which is written for an MA(1) remainder, a shock that lingers one period (Baltagi 2021, 125–26). The tests of section 4 are all ways of telling the floor from the band. The limit of the band is a unit root in the error, \(\rho = 1\), a shock that never fades (why unit roots matter); that case is the boundary this chapter hands to dynamic panels.

Two transformations of the chapter leave their own fingerprint, and the tests have to know it. Taking each unit’s data in deviation from its mean, the within transformation, turns uncorrelated remainders into residuals with correlation \(-1/(T-1)\) between any two years; first differencing turns them into residuals with correlation \(-0.5\) between neighbours and zero beyond (Wooldridge 2010; Croissant and Millo 2019, 101, 103). A test of “no serial correlation” on within residuals is therefore a test against \(-1/(T-1)\), not against zero.

K.3 2. Same slopes

Chow’s F fits one regression to everyone and one to each unit and compares residual sums of squares. With \(N\) units, \(K' = K + 1\) coefficients each, the restricted residual sum \(e'e\) and the unrestricted one \(\sum_i e_i'e_i\),

\[ F = \frac{(e'e - \sum_i e_i'e_i)/(N-1)K'}{\sum_i e_i'e_i / N(T-K')} \;\sim\; F_{(N-1)K',\, N(T-K')} \]

under \(H_0: \beta_i = \beta\) for all i and spherical errors, \(u \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I_{NT})\) (Chow 1960; Baltagi 2021, eq. 4.8, p. 77). Restricting the slopes only, with the within fit as the restricted model, gives the same statistic on \((N-1)K\) and \(N(T-K-1)\) degrees of freedom (Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 8.2.3, p. 192). Pooling across years instead of units swaps the roles of N and T.

Spherical errors is the catch. Under a random unit effect the Chow F “is not the right test to perform”: transform the model by \(\Omega^{-1/2}\) first, so that the errors become spherical, then run the Chow F on the transformed data. That is the Roy–Zellner test, operational once \(\Omega\) is replaced by a consistent estimate (Roy 1957; Zellner 1962; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 4.9–4.14, pp. 77–79). Baltagi’s Monte Carlo on a two-way model with poolable data found the plain Chow test rejecting the true null with “a high frequency” whenever the variance components were not small, and the Roy–Zellner test holding its size (Baltagi 1981; 2021, 79). On Grunfeld the two tests agree, 27.75 and 4.35 on F(27, 170), both rejections (Baltagi 2021, 80); the point is the size, not the verdict. And a rejection is a statement about bias, not about what to report: “imposing the pooling restriction reduces the variance of the pooled estimator but may introduce bias if these restrictions are false” (Croissant and Millo 2019, 186), which is why the chapter shows the spread of the individual slopes beside the pooled one.

Swamy’s random coefficients make the spread a parameter. Let each unit’s coefficient vector be a draw around a common mean,

\[ y_i = Z_i \beta_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \beta_i = \beta + \eta_i, \quad \eta_i \sim (0, \Delta), \]

so that the error of unit i becomes \(\varepsilon_i + Z_i \eta_i\) with covariance \(\sigma^2_i I_T + Z_i \Delta Z_i'\): heteroskedastic across units and correlated within them (Swamy 1970; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 8.2.2, p. 187). Swamy’s estimator of the spread subtracts the sampling noise of the unit-by-unit OLS slopes from their empirical covariance,

\[ \hat\Delta = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_i (\hat\beta_i - \bar\beta)(\hat\beta_i - \bar\beta)' - \frac{1}{N} \sum_i \hat\sigma^2_i (Z_i'Z_i)^{-1}, \]

and GLS with the implied covariance is the Swamy estimator of \(\beta\) (Croissant and Millo 2019, 188). The subtraction can leave \(\hat\Delta\) not positive definite. The mean-group estimator, the plain average of the unit slopes with the empirical covariance of the \(\hat\beta_i\) as its dispersion (common correlated effects), is Swamy’s with equal weights, “always non-negative definite”, and the two converge as T grows (Croissant and Millo 2019, 190). plm::pvcm(model = "random") returns \(\hat\Delta\) in $Delta.

Swamy also gave a test of \(\beta_i = \beta\): the weighted dispersion of the unit slopes around the pooled estimator,

\[ \hat S = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (\hat\beta_i - \hat\beta_{WFE})' \frac{X_i' M_\tau X_i}{\hat\sigma^2_i} (\hat\beta_i - \hat\beta_{WFE}), \]

which is \(\chi^2_{k(N-1)}\) as \(T \to \infty\) with N fixed; it was built for panels “where N is small relative to T” (Swamy 1970; Pesaran 2015, eq. 28.78, pp. 737–738). With 49 states and 29 years, or 10 firms and 20, the fixed-N asymptotics are not the ones at hand. Pesaran and Yamagata standardise the same dispersion so that it is normal as N and T both grow,

\[ \hat\Delta = \sqrt N \, \frac{N^{-1}\hat S - k}{\sqrt{2k}}, \qquad \tilde\Delta = \sqrt N \, \frac{N^{-1}\tilde S - k}{\sqrt{2k}}, \]

where \(\tilde S\) replaces each \(\hat\sigma^2_i\) by a variance computed from the pooled fixed-effects residuals. Both tend to \(N(0, 1)\) under the null, \(\hat\Delta\) if \(\sqrt N / T \to 0\) and \(\tilde\Delta\) under the weaker \(\sqrt N / T^2 \to 0\); under normal errors the mean-and-variance adjusted versions are valid with no restriction on how N and T grow (Pesaran and Yamagata 2008; Pesaran 2015, eq. 28.79, pp. 738–739). The price is in the assumptions: errors independent across units and years and homoskedastic within a unit, regressors strictly exogenous (Pesaran 2015, 739). Every other section of this note is about panels that break at least one, which is why Blomquist and Westerlund’s heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation robust version exists (Blomquist and Westerlund 2013). Neither test is implemented in R; Stata’s xthst is the reference implementation (Bersvendsen and Ditzen 2021), and the chapter states this as a limitation. A Hausman-type contrast of the fixed-effects and mean-group slopes is the other candidate, and it lacks power in every direction when the slopes are random draws, which is exactly Swamy’s alternative (Pesaran and Yamagata 2008; Baltagi 2021, 101).

K.4 3. Same variance

Big units have big shocks. Heteroskedasticity, unequal error variances, is the rule “where the cross-sectional units may be of varying size”, and it leaves the slopes consistent but not efficient while the classical standard errors “will be biased” (Baltagi 2021, 109). In a panel it can sit in either component: Mazodier and Trognon put it in the unit effect, \(\alpha_i \sim (0, w_i^2)\), which gives each unit its own quasi-demeaning fraction \(\theta_i\) (Mazodier and Trognon 1978); the alternative puts it in the remainder, \(\varepsilon_{it} \sim (0, w_i^2)\); and “it is hard to claim that it is in one component and not the other” (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 5.1–5.7, pp. 109–111).

The tests are the gap. Verbon’s Lagrange multiplier test takes both variances to be the same parametric function of time-invariant unit characteristics \(Z_i\) (Verbon 1980; Baltagi 2021, 113). Lejeune’s tests let \(\sigma^2_{\varepsilon,it}\) and \(\sigma^2_{\alpha,i}\) be distinct functions of exogenous variables and test homoskedasticity jointly with the absence of unit effects, so that OLS is the restricted model, once for a random and once for a fixed effect (Lejeune 2006; Baltagi 2021, 113–14). Neither has an R implementation, and no panel test of homoskedasticity does; the chapter shows the fan of residual spread against unit size and runs a cross-section test on the within-transformed data as a substitute, labelled as one.

The repair that needs no test is Arellano’s variance for the within estimator. Stack each unit’s \(T\) within-transformed observations, allow any \(T \times T\) covariance \(\Sigma_i\) inside a unit and none across units, and estimate the middle of White’s sandwich with the unit’s own residual outer product:

\[ \widehat{\mathrm{var}}(\hat\beta_W) = (\tilde X'\tilde X)^{-1} \Big[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde X_i' \tilde u_i \tilde u_i' \tilde X_i \Big] (\tilde X'\tilde X)^{-1} \]

(Arellano 1987; White 1980; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 2.13–2.16, pp. 18–19). It is robust to heteroskedasticity and to serial correlation of any form within a unit, and its asymptotics are \(N \to \infty\) with T fixed (Baltagi 2021, 18). The plain White estimator on the within fit, which allows only the variances to differ, is inconsistent for fixed T (Baltagi 2021, 19), and it understates the problem on every panel in the chapter. When T is large as well, consistency needs either a limit on the serial correlation or \(T/N \to 0\) (Pesaran 2015, sec. 26.7, p. 655).

The repair that recovers efficiency is feasible GLS on the whole \(T \times T\) block. Assume \(\Omega = I_N \otimes \Sigma_T\) with \(\Sigma_T\) unrestricted but the same for every unit, estimate it from the cross-section,

\[ \hat\Sigma_T = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat e_i \hat e_i', \]

and run GLS with it: the general GLS of Wooldridge, also called the Parks estimator, a variant of Zellner’s SUR (Parks 1967; Zellner 1962; Wooldridge 2010; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 5.2.1, pp. 127–128). It allows any within-unit heteroskedasticity and serial correlation, rules out cross-sectional correlation, and needs \(N \gg T\) because it estimates \(T(T+1)/2\) covariances from N residual vectors (Croissant and Millo 2019, 128). On a wide short panel it is cheap; on ten firms over twenty years it is 210 parameters from ten observations, and the chapter shows that fit only to read it as unreliable. Driscoll and Kraay note that the shortage of degrees of freedom makes the estimate near-singular and the standard errors downward biased; Beck and Katz document the same small-sample bias in the pooled time series it was first used on (Driscoll and Kraay 1998; Beck and Katz 1995; Croissant and Millo 2019, 128). On within-transformed data the same recipe is Kiefer’s fixed-effects GLS, with one period dropped because the demeaned residual covariance has rank \(T - 1\) (Kiefer 1980; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 5.2.1.2, p. 131); on first differences it is first-difference GLS, the better choice when the fixed-effects residuals are not stationary (Croissant and Millo 2019, 132). plm::pggls fits all three, and the estimated \(\hat\Sigma_T\) in $sigma is itself a picture of the error: off-diagonals that stay flat with distance are a unit effect, off-diagonals that shrink with distance are an AR(1) (Croissant and Millo 2019, 129).

Heteroskedasticity has one more consequence in this book: under it the maximum-likelihood spatial estimators of chapter 03 are inconsistent, and the GM fit is the one to keep (spatial fixed effects; (Lin and Lee 2010; Pesaran 2015, 807)).

K.5 4. No memory

Every test here has to decide what to do about the unit effect, and the answer sorts them into three kinds: joint tests of both departures at once, marginal tests of one that assume the other away, and conditional tests of one that allow the other at any size (Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 4.3.2, pp. 96–97). A marginal test for serial correlation that assumes no unit effect will reject because of the unit effect; a joint test rejects for either reason and does not say which.

Baltagi and Li derived the joint Lagrange multiplier test from pooled OLS residuals \(\hat u\). With \(A = \hat u'(I_N \otimes J_T)\hat u / \hat u'\hat u - 1\), the Breusch–Pagan ingredient, and \(B = \hat u'\hat u_{-1} / \hat u'\hat u\), the first-order autocorrelation of the stacked residuals,

\[ LM_1 = \frac{NT^2}{2(T-1)(T-2)} \left[ A^2 - 4AB + 2TB^2 \right] \;\sim\; \chi^2_2 \]

under \(H_0: \sigma^2_\alpha = 0,\ \rho = 0\), and the statistic is the same whether the alternative is AR(1) or MA(1) (Baltagi and Li 1991, 1995; Baltagi 2021, eq. 5.36, pp. 125–126). The \(A^2\) term alone is the Breusch–Pagan test, \(LM_2 = \sqrt{NT/2(T-1)}\, A\) in Honda’s one-sided form; the \(B^2\) term alone is the Breusch–Godfrey test on the stacked residuals, \(LM_3 = \sqrt{NT^2/(T-1)}\, B\), which assumes no unit effect (Breusch and Pagan 1980; Honda 1985; Breusch 1978; Godfrey 1978; Baltagi 2021, 126). The cross term \(-4AB\) is why the joint test is needed when both are suspected. And \(LM_3\) is the one not to use: with a unit effect present “one is very likely to reject the null hypothesis of \(H_3\) even if the null is true. This is because the \(\mu_i\) are correlated for the same individual across time, and this will contribute to rejecting the null of no serial correlation” (Baltagi 2021, 127). On Grunfeld it is 143.5 against the locally robust value 10.3 (Baltagi 2021, Table 5.3, p. 129) and the conditional value 69.5 that the chapter prints; both tests are defined below.

Two families fix the marginal tests. The conditional test \(LM_4\) tests \(\rho = 0\) given \(\sigma^2_\alpha > 0\), from the residuals of the random-effects model estimated by maximum likelihood, and is again the same for AR(1) and MA(1) (Baltagi and Li 1995; Baltagi 2021, 127); it is plm::pbltest, and it is the optimal test when a random effect is taken for granted (Croissant and Millo 2019, 97). Bera, Sosa-Escudero and Yoon keep the OLS residuals and correct each marginal statistic for its asymptotic correlation with the other score,

\[ LM^*_\alpha = \frac{NT\,(2B - A)^2}{2(T-1)(1 - 2/T)}, \qquad LM^*_\rho = \frac{NT^2\,[B - A/T]^2}{(T-1)(1 - 2/T)}, \]

each \(\chi^2_1\) under its null and robust to a small amount of the other departure; the four pieces add up, \(LM^*_\alpha + LM_\rho = LM^*_\rho + LM_\alpha = LM_1\), so the joint statistic decomposes into an adjusted test of one direction plus the unadjusted test of the other (Bera et al. 2001; Baltagi 2021, 128). These are plm::pbsytest with test = "re", "ar" and "j", and Stata’s xttest1 prints the whole family: on Grunfeld, 798.16 and 28.25 for the unit effect, 664.95 and 25.79 adjusted; 143.52 for serial correlation unadjusted and 10.31 adjusted; 808.47 joint (Baltagi 2021, Table 5.3, p. 129). All of them assume normal, homoskedastic errors (Croissant and Millo 2019, 97).

The fixed-effects test uses within residuals \(\tilde\varepsilon_{it}\), whose unit effect is gone:

\[ LM_5 = \sqrt{\frac{NT^2}{T-1}} \; \frac{\tilde\varepsilon' \tilde\varepsilon_{-1}} {\tilde\varepsilon' \tilde\varepsilon} \;\sim\; N(0, 1) \]

one-sided, for large T (Baltagi and Li 1995; Baltagi 2021, eq. 5.43, p. 130). It differs from \(LM_3\) only in the residuals, and “since the Within transformation wipes out the individual effects whether fixed or random, one can also use (5.43) to test for serial correlation in the random effects models” (Baltagi 2021, 130). Nothing in plm is literally this statistic; plm::pbgtest on a within fit is its Breusch–Godfrey cousin, and the induced \(-1/(T-1)\) of section 1 biases both toward rejection when T is short (Croissant and Millo 2019, 101). The Bhargava, Franzini and Narendranathan Durbin–Watson is the same residuals in Durbin–Watson form,

\[ d_p = \frac{\sum_i \sum_{t=2}^{T} (\tilde\varepsilon_{it} - \tilde\varepsilon_{i,t-1})^2} {\sum_i \sum_{t=1}^{T} \tilde\varepsilon_{it}^2}, \]

locally most powerful invariant near \(\rho = 0\), with tabulated bounds for balanced panels and, for very large N, the rule of simply asking whether \(d_p < 2\) (Bhargava et al. 1982; Baltagi 2021, eq. 5.44, p. 130). Baltagi and Wu’s locally best invariant statistic extends it to unequally spaced panels (Baltagi and Wu 1999; Baltagi 2021, 121); plm::pbnftest returns both without a p-value, as Stata does (Baltagi 2021, 130). Inoue and Solon’s portmanteau test of all \((T-1)(T-2)/2\) autocorrelations at once needs \(N \gg T^2/2\) and is not run here (Inoue and Solon 2006; Baltagi 2021, 131).

Wooldridge’s tests need no normality. The unobserved-effects test averages the off-diagonal products of a unit’s pooled residuals, scales by their standard deviation, and is \(N(0, 1)\) as N grows under any error distribution; it has power against a unit effect and against serial correlation alike, “trading some power against more specific alternatives in exchange for robustness”, and a rejection “should not be interpreted as implying that the random effects error structure must be true” (Wooldridge 2010; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 4.3.1, pp. 95–96). The within-based test regresses within residuals on their lag, with an intercept,

\[ \tilde\varepsilon_{it} = \delta + \phi\, \tilde\varepsilon_{i,t-1} + \xi_{it}, \qquad H_0: \phi = -\tfrac{1}{T-1}, \]

with a cluster-robust covariance, so it is valid for short T and large N (Wooldridge 2010; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 4.3.5.1, p. 102). The first-difference test regresses differenced residuals on their lag and tests \(\phi = -0.5\), the value uncorrelated original errors imply; Drukker’s Monte Carlo documents its size and power (Drukker 2003; Wooldridge 2010; Croissant and Millo 2019, 103). Run both ways, h0 = "fe" and h0 = "fd", it is a choice of estimator: if the original errors are white noise, within is efficient and the differenced errors show \(-0.5\); if the original errors are a random walk, the differenced errors are white noise and first differences are efficient (Wooldridge 2010; Baltagi 2021, 22–23; Croissant and Millo 2019, 103). “Not rejecting one of the two is evidence in favor of using the estimator corresponding to h0”; when both reject, “the truth clearly lies in the middle” and “whichever estimator is chosen will have serially correlated errors”, which is the case for robust standard errors (Croissant and Millo 2019, 104).

K.6 5. The sandwich and its building blocks

Every robust covariance in the chapter is one formula. For a regression with regressors \(X\) and residuals \(\hat u\), the variance of the OLS slopes with a general error covariance is \((X'X)^{-1} (X'\Omega X) (X'X)^{-1}\), and White’s insight is that the middle, the meat, needs only \(K(K+1)/2\) consistent numbers, not the \(NT(NT+1)/2\) in \(\Omega\) (White 1980; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 5.1.1, p. 110). The panel estimators differ only in which residual products they let into the meat (Millo 2017):

\[ \begin{aligned} V_{wh} &= (X'X)^{-1} \Big[ \sum_i \sum_t \hat u_{it}^2\, x_{it} x_{it}' \Big] (X'X)^{-1}, \\ V_{cx} &= (X'X)^{-1} \Big[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i' \hat u_i \hat u_i' X_i \Big] (X'X)^{-1}, \\ V_{ct} &= (X'X)^{-1} \Big[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} X_t' \hat u_t \hat u_t' X_t \Big] (X'X)^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]

\(V_{wh}\) allows the variances to differ and nothing else (Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.4, p. 111). \(V_{cx}\) clusters by unit: it allows any heteroskedasticity and any serial correlation inside a unit, assumes units independent, and is Arellano’s estimator of section 3, Liang and Zeger’s in the statistics literature; its asymptotics are in N, the number of clusters (Arellano 1987; Liang and Zeger 1986; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.2, p. 111). \(V_{ct}\) clusters by year: any cross-sectional correlation inside a year, years independent, asymptotics in T (Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.5, p. 111). “As a general rule, the estimator is asymptotic in the number of clusters” (Croissant and Millo 2019, 110): ten firms are ten clusters, and the Arellano standard errors on Grunfeld are noisy for that reason.

Double clustering adds the two and subtracts the diagonal that both counted,

\[ V_{cxt} = V_{cx} + V_{ct} - V_{wh}, \]

robust to any dependence within a unit and within a year at once, with the smaller of the two cluster counts having to grow (Cameron et al. 2011; Thompson 2011; Petersen 2009; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.6, p. 115). It rules out correlation between different units in different years. The kernel estimators let that in, with weights that fall with the lag. Panel Newey–West adds to \(V_{wh}\) the lagged products of each unit’s own residuals,

\[ V_{nw,L} = V_{wh} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l \big[ V_{wh,l} + V_{wh,l}' \big], \]

with Bartlett weights \(w_l = 1 - l/(L+1)\) and \(L\) of the order of \(T^{1/4}\) (Newey and West 1987; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.10, pp. 110, 116, 119). Driscoll–Kraay does the same to the year-clustered estimator,

\[ V_{scc,L} = V_{ct} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l \big[ V_{ct,l} + V_{ct,l}' \big], \]

so that cross-sectional correlation within a year and cross-serial correlation between any two units up to \(L\) years apart are both covered (Driscoll and Kraay 1998; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.11, p. 116). Its price is the one the spatial chapter’s limits already met: Driscoll and Kraay’s conditions deliver standard errors “robust to general forms of spatial and temporal dependence as \(T \to \infty\). However, if T is small, they conclude that the problem of consistent nonparametric covariance matrix estimation is much less tractable” (Baltagi 2021, 394); the practical minimum from their Monte Carlo is \(T > 20\)–\(25\), and N can grow at any rate (Croissant and Millo 2019, 116). Seventeen and twenty years sit at that floor. Beck and Katz’s panel-corrected standard errors take a different route to the same dependence: assume the \(N \times N\) cross-sectional covariance is the same in every year, estimate it as \(\sum_t \hat u_t \hat u_t' / T\), and plug it into the sandwich; robust to cross-sectional heteroskedasticity and correlation, and only to them (Beck and Katz 1995; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.13, p. 122).

Millo’s comprehensive formula holds all of these,

\[ V_{cxt,L} = V_{ct} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l [V_{ct,l} + V_{ct,l}'] + V_{cx} - V_{wh} - \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l [V_{wh,l} + V_{wh,l}'], \]

and each named estimator is a choice of which terms to keep and which weights to use: \(L = 0\) gives double clustering, dropping \(V_{cx} - V_{wh}\) and its lags gives Driscoll–Kraay, \(w_l = 1\) gives Thompson’s double clustering with persistent shocks (Millo 2017; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 5.12 and Table 5.1, p. 117). plm::vcovG builds one block, vcovHC, vcovSCC, vcovNW and vcovDC are the named sums, and the chapter’s se_table() lays the nine out side by side after every call has been shown. The blocks are additive, so the table reads as a diagnosis: the gap between \(V_{wh}\) and \(V_{cx}\) is serial correlation within a unit, the gap between \(V_{cx}\) and \(V_{cxt}\) is what clustering by year adds, and a kernel estimator that comes out below the clustered one is not reassurance but bias, because the kernel “unduly downweighs the covariance between faraway observations” when the dependence is a persistent unit effect (Petersen 2009; Croissant and Millo 2019, 119–21).

Two cautions on transformed data. The within transformation induces the \(-1/(T-1)\) correlation of section 1, so clustering by unit with unit effects is right, clustering by year with time effects is right, and “the combination of group clustering with time fixed effects and the reverse is inappropriate” (Croissant and Millo 2019, 121). And a persistent unit effect left in the residuals biases every kernel estimator, which is one reason to demean or difference before applying one (Croissant and Millo 2019, 121).

K.7 If / then

If Then
Chow’s F rejects but the errors have a unit effect The plain F over-rejects; read the Roy–Zellner F on the GLS-transformed data (Baltagi 2021, 79)
The slopes differ and N is large against T Swamy’s \(\chi^2\) needs \(T \to \infty\) at fixed N; the Δ tests are the right ones and are not in R (Pesaran 2015, 737–39)
\(\hat\Delta\) is not positive definite Report the mean-group dispersion, which always is (Croissant and Millo 2019, 190)
Residual spread grows with unit size Slopes stay consistent; replace the classical errors by Arellano’s, or reweight by FGLS if \(N \gg T\) (Baltagi 2021, 18–19, 109; Croissant and Millo 2019, 128)
Only the unit effect is in doubt Breusch–Pagan or Honda; \(LM_3\) never, since the unit effect makes it reject (Baltagi 2021, 127)
Both departures are suspected The joint \(LM_1\), then the BSY adjusted pair to say which (Baltagi 2021, 128)
A random effect is taken as given The conditional \(LM_4\) from the ML fit (Croissant and Millo 2019, 97)
The model is fixed effects Within residuals: \(LM_5\), BFN’s \(d_p\), or Wooldridge’s within test against \(-1/(T-1)\) (Baltagi 2021, 130; Croissant and Millo 2019, 102)
h0 = "fe" rejects and h0 = "fd" does not First differences; the original errors look like a random walk (Croissant and Millo 2019, 103–4)
Both legs of the FD test reject Either estimator with a serial-correlation-robust covariance (Croissant and Millo 2019, 104)
Dependence is within units only Cluster by unit; asymptotics in N (Croissant and Millo 2019, 111)
Dependence is within years and within units Double clustering; both counts must be large (Croissant and Millo 2019, 115)
Units are correlated across years too, and \(T > 20\)–\(25\) Driscoll–Kraay (Croissant and Millo 2019, 116; Baltagi 2021, 394)
A kernel estimator is smaller than the clustered one The unit effect is persistent and the kernel is biased; trust the cluster (Croissant and Millo 2019, 121)

K.8 Open

  • Pesaran–Yamagata Δ and its HAC extension have no R implementation; Stata’s xthst is the benchmark, and the chapter defers it (Bersvendsen and Ditzen 2021; Blomquist and Westerlund 2013).
  • Verbon’s and Lejeune’s tests of homoskedasticity, and the joint LM test of Baltagi, Bresson and Pirotte that nests them, are not implemented; the chapter has a picture and a substitute, not a test with a published size (Baltagi et al. 2006; Baltagi 2021, 113–14).
  • \(LM_5\) is not literally in plm; pbnftest and pbgtest on within residuals are the nearest (Baltagi 2021, 130).
  • Bester, Conley and Hansen’s group-membership HAC estimator is not implemented; its validity “relies on the capacity of the researcher to construct groups whose averages are approximately uncorrelated”, and plm::cortab checks that precondition and nothing more (Bester et al. 2011; Pesaran 2015, 814).
  • Inoue–Solon needs \(N \gg T^2/2\) and is not run on any panel here (Baltagi 2021, 131).

K.9 Next

When the errors are nearer a random walk than white noise, the regression itself is the problem, not its standard errors, and the lagged outcome belongs on the right-hand side: dynamic panel GMM. The unit effect that every test here had to allow for is the subject of fixed and random effects.
