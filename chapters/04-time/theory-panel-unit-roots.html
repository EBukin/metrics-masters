Appendix H — Panel unit roots: what a rejection is worth

The three alternatives, and size before power

Code: state the alternative first, first-generation tests, second-generation tests, the fraction of stationary series, size under spatial dependence, cross-unit cointegration, cointegration and the long run. Related notes: cross-sectional dependence and common factors and CCE.

H.1 Why pool at all

A single unit-root test on 20 or 30 annual observations has almost no power: the Dickey–Fuller distribution is wide, and a root of 0.9 looks much like a root of 1 over that span. Panel tests buy power by adding units instead of years. N series of length T carry more evidence against a unit root than one series of length T, provided the series bring independent evidence.

That proviso is the whole subject of this note. The extra power comes from averaging, and averaging only helps when the things averaged are not the same thing counted twice. Everything below is a consequence of asking what is being averaged, and over what.

H.2 1. One null, three alternatives

Every test in plm::purtest and plm::cipstest, except Hadri’s, shares one null. Write each unit’s ADF regression as

\[\Delta y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i y_{i,t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p_i} \gamma_{ij} \Delta y_{i,t-j} + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad \beta_i = \rho_i - 1.\]

The null is \(H_0: \beta_i = 0\) for every i. The tests differ in what they claim when they reject (Pesaran 2015, 818–20, eqs. 31.4–31.6):

Alternative In words Tests
H₁ₐ \(\beta_i = \beta < 0\) for all i one common stationary root LLC, Harris–Tzavalis, Breitung
H₁b \(\beta_i < 0\) for \(N_1\) units, \(N_1/N \to \kappa > 0\) a share of the panel is stationary IPS, Fisher-type, CIPS
H₁c \(\beta_i < 0\) for a fixed number of units, \(\kappa = 0\) a handful is stationary none

H₁ₐ is strong: it says every unit reverts, and at the same speed. Few economists believe that of 49 states or 170 regions, which is why the heterogeneous alternative H₁b became the default after Im et al. (2003).

H₁c is the case no averaging test can see. With a fixed count of stationary units, their signal is divided by an N that keeps growing. The simulation in the examples shows it: IPS power against two stationary series falls from 0.40 to 0.25 as N goes from 10 to 90, while power against a 40% share rises to 1.

Important

The printed p-value does not carry the alternative. purtest labels the alternative “stationarity” for LLC and IPS alike, though LLC rejects towards H₁ₐ and IPS towards H₁b. The alternative has to be written into the text by hand, every time a panel unit-root result is reported.

Remark 8 of Pesaran (2015, 820) sets the limit on that text. When T is short — around 15 — a panel rejection is informative only “in an average sense”. It says the panel, taken as a whole, does not look like N random walks. It does not say which units are stationary.

H.3 2. How the first generation builds a statistic

Each first-generation test runs the ADF regression above on every unit and then combines the results. They differ only in what they combine.

Family What it combines Tests in plm Source
Pooled one \(\hat\beta\) from all units stacked "levinlin" Levin et al. (2002)
Mean of t the average of the N ADF t statistics "ips" Im et al. (2003)
Fisher the N individual p-values "madwu", "Pm", "invnormal", "logit" Maddala and Wu (1999), Choi (2001)
Reversed null the N KPSS statistics "hadri" Hadri (2000)

Pooled. LLC stacks all units and estimates one \(\beta\). Pooling only makes sense if \(\beta\) is common, so LLC’s alternative is H₁ₐ by construction. The pooled t is biased because each unit has its own intercept and lag structure, and Levin et al. (2002) correct it with tabulated mean and variance adjustments (Baltagi 2021, 340–43). Harris–Tzavalis (Harris and Tzavalis 1999) and Breitung (Breitung 2000) are in the same family. Neither is in plm.

Mean of t. IPS averages the individual ADF statistics, \(\bar t = N^{-1}\sum_i t_i\), then standardises (Baltagi 2021, 344–45). Each unit keeps its own \(\beta_i\), so the alternative is H₁b. The unstandardised tbar is exact for fixed T but is read against tabulated critical values, and only without lags.

Fisher. Maddala and Wu (1999) combine the individual p-values instead of the statistics: under the null, \(-2\sum_i \ln p_i \sim \chi^2_{2N}\). Choi (2001) adds three variants. "invnormal" sums \(\Phi^{-1}(p_i)\), "logit" sums \(\ln[p_i/(1-p_i)]\), and "Pm" standardises the Maddala–Wu statistic so that it stays valid as \(N \to \infty\). The Fisher family needs neither a balanced panel nor equal lag lengths, which is why it runs on EmplUK where LLC refuses (Baltagi 2021, 346–48).

A Fisher test is only as good as its p-values, and those come from the non-standard Dickey–Fuller distribution. plm reads them off the response surfaces of MacKinnon (1996) through urca (Pfaff 2008). Without urca it falls back to the older approximation of MacKinnon (1994). On US house prices the switch moves the Maddala–Wu p-value from 0.4292 to 0.4313 — visible in the fourth decimal, irrelevant to a reading.

Reversed null. Hadri (2000) tests the opposite hypothesis: every series is stationary, against a unit root in at least one. It is the panel version of the KPSS test (Kwiatkowski et al. 1992), an LM statistic averaged over units. Its value is corroboration. When LLC, IPS and Maddala–Wu fail to reject a unit root and Hadri rejects stationarity, two tests with opposite nulls point the same way. That is the pattern on log house prices: p = 0.10, 0.43 and 0.78 for the first three, p ≈ 0 for Hadri.

Lag length. Every test except Hadri needs the \(p_i\) lags of the ADF regression. purtest fixes them by hand or chooses them per unit by AIC, SIC, or the general-to-specific rule of Hall (1994). Too few lags leave serial correlation in \(\varepsilon_{it}\) and over-reject. Too many waste the short T the panel was meant to compensate for.

H.4 3. First and second generation: one assumption

“Second generation” is not a date. It is one assumption dropped: that units are independent once a common time effect is removed.

The first generation allows exactly one form of dependence. Subtract the cross-section mean each period and a common shock that hits every unit equally disappears. That is the two-way fixed-effects identity of cross-sectional dependence: a time effect is a factor model with every loading equal to one. If the loadings differ, demeaning leaves the factor in the residuals and every unit’s evidence is partly the same evidence.

Pesaran’s CADF regression (Pesaran 2007) allows heterogeneous loadings. It adds the cross-section averages \(\bar y_{t-1}\) and \(\Delta \bar y_t\) to each unit’s ADF equation:

\[\Delta y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i y_{i,t-1} + c_i \bar y_{t-1} + d_i \Delta \bar y_t + \dots + e_{it}.\]

The averages proxy the single unobserved factor, and each unit gets its own coefficients on them, so each unit gets its own loading. CIPS is the mean of the resulting t statistics — IPS on defactored regressions (Baltagi 2021, 352–53). The idea is the same one that drives CCE estimation, in common factors and CCE.

plm::cipstest exposes all three levels of this argument through model:

model = Regression Handles
"mg" plain ADF per unit, averaged independence only — this is IPS
"dmg" ADF on cross-sectionally demeaned data one factor, equal loadings
"cmg" (default) CADF, with cross-section averages one factor, any loadings

Nothing in plm handles more than one factor. Pesaran et al. (2013) extend CIPS by adding further cross-section averages of other variables, and add the CSB statistic (Pesaran 2015, 836). Moon–Perron (Moon and Perron 2004), Bai–Ng’s PANIC (Bai and Ng 2004) and Phillips–Sul (Phillips and Sul 2003) defactor by principal components first and test afterwards. None has an R implementation in this project.

CIPS has a cost: its distribution is non-standard and depends on N, T and the deterministic terms. cipstest returns the nearest tabulated significance level, not an exact p-value. A printed p-value = 0.1 means above 0.10, and 0.01 means below 0.01.

H.5 4. Size before power

Every comparative ranking of these tests in the textbooks is a ranking of power — and power only means something when the test rejects a true null at its nominal rate. Both standard rankings assume that:

  • LLC has higher local power than IPS (Pesaran 2015, 819);
  • LLC and Breitung have the smallest size distortions, and Fisher does at least as well as IPS (Pesaran 2015, 838; Baltagi 2021, 348).

Two things break size in a large-N, short-T panel, and they compound.

The shape of the panel. LLC’s asymptotics need T to grow faster than N (Levin et al. 2002; Baltagi 2021, 340–43). On independent random walks, with no dependence at all, the examples’ simulation gives LLC a size of 0.09 at N/T = 0.04 and 0.855 at N/T = 3.33. IPS is better but not right: 0.085 to 0.15 at T = 30. The target shape of this project is N/T ≈ 8.5, off the end of that table.

Cross-sectional dependence. When units move together, N series are worth fewer than N independent ones, and a test that counts them as N over-rejects. Baltagi et al. (2007) ran 1600 experiments under spatial error dependence: distortions are largest under a spatial autoregressive error and grow with the density of W (Baltagi 2021, 353–54).

Pesaran (2015, 839) draws two rules from that literature, and they pull in opposite directions:

  • The CD-based approach (CIPS) is the most robust to spatial alternatives.
  • Factor-based corrections are invalid under weak dependence, because they remove a “factor” that is not there.

In the project’s own simulation, on a 7 × 7 rook W, CIPS held its size: 0.04 at λ = 0 and 0.07 at λ = 0.8. The four first-generation tests rejected a true null two and a half to twelve times too often even at λ = 0. Shape, not space, did most of the damage.

Warning

At N/T ≫ 1, a first-generation rejection is not evidence. It is what those tests do to random walks at that shape. Report CIPS, and read every first-generation p-value as an upper bound on the evidence rather than a measurement of it.

H.6 5. What a rejection licenses

Under H₁b, a rejection means some share of units is stationary. It does not say which, and it is not a licence to treat the panel as stationary and pool it.

Hanck (2013) turns the rejection into a list. Take the individual p-values a Fisher-type test already computes, sort them, and apply two classical multiple-testing results:

  • Simes’ test (Simes 1986) of the intersection null. With sorted p-values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(N)}\), reject when \(\min_j N p_{(j)}/j \le \alpha\). Unlike the Fisher combination, it stays valid under general cross-sectional dependence.
  • Hommel’s procedure (Hommel 1988) for the individual hypotheses. It controls the family-wise error rate, so each unit is judged on its own without the multiplicity of N tests inflating false rejections.

plm::phansitest implements both. It accepts any purtest object with per-series p-values (madwu, Pm, invnormal, logit, ips) and refuses Hadri, whose statistic is not built from individual p-values.

The two cases in the examples show both outcomes:

Panel Simes global p Units rejected (Hommel)
19 real exchange rates (Hanck 2013, Table 11) 0.0003 10 of 19
49 US state house prices 0.12 0 of 49

The exchange-rate panel rejects the intersection null and names the ten stationary countries — the two answers Remark 8 asks for. The house-price panel rejects neither. Note that its Simes p-value of 0.12 is far sharper than the Maddala–Wu p-value of 0.43 on the same data.

H.7 6. The assumption no test checks

Cross-sectional correlation is units moving together. Cross-unit cointegration is stronger: a linear combination of different units’ series is stationary, so the units share a stochastic trend. Pesaran (2015, 836) warns that panel unit-root tests may be “severely biased” under the second while tolerating the first.

CADF allows one common I(1) factor — that is what the cross-section averages absorb — and rules out further cointegration among what is left (Pesaran 2015, 837). The subsampling test of Choi and Chue (2007) allows it but needs N small relative to T (Pesaran 2015, 838), the opposite of a spatial panel.

Correlation does not reveal it. In the examples, a cointegrated pair and a pair with correlated increments have correlations of 0.90 and 0.77. A Johansen rank test separates them perfectly; no CD statistic or α could. So every CIPS result in this book holds under an assumption the book cannot test.

H.8 7. If / then

If Then
CD rejects, or α is near 1 First-generation tests are invalid. Use CIPS.
N/T well above 1 First-generation size is broken even without dependence. Use CIPS.
CIPS rejects A share of units is stationary (H₁b). Name them with phansitest.
CIPS does not reject Treat the panel as I(1). Corroborate with Hadri’s reversed null.
Dependence is weak and spatial, not factor-driven CIPS defactors needlessly. Its size survived λ ≤ 0.8 here, but that is not a general licence.
Several common factors are plausible Not testable in this book. State it as a limitation.
The text says “the panel is stationary” Rewrite it. Say which alternative was tested, and how many units.

H.9 8. Open

  • Missing tests. Breitung and Harris–Tzavalis — the two tests with the best size record in the comparisons — are not in purtest. The benchmark is Stata’s xtunitroot.
  • Several factors. CIPS with multiple factors and CSB (Pesaran et al. 2013), Moon–Perron and PANIC have no R implementation and no verification target.
  • Cross-unit cointegration. No panel test exists in R. The assumption has to be carried as a stated limitation into cointegration in panels.
  • The target shape. No published size table covers N ≈ 170, T ≈ 20 with a spatial W. The examples’ Monte Carlo is the only evidence, and it covers this code on one W.
  • A software trap. In plm 2.6.7, the print method of phansitest says “H0 rejected (globally)” whatever the result. Read $rejected.no and compute Simes’ p-value directly.

H.10 Next

With the unit-root question settled for a panel, the next one is whether its I(1) series are cointegrated. Cointegration in panels covers it, with the code in cointegration and the long run; the only verifiable route is the CCE-plus-CIPS substitute of the second-generation section.
