Appendix I — Cointegration in panels

One trend shared by two series, and what a panel adds

Code: cointegration and estimating the long run. Related notes: panel unit roots and common factors and CCE.

I.1 Why the method exists

Log house prices and log income in a state both wander: each has a unit root, a shock that never fades, so neither returns to a fixed level (why it matters). Regress one on the other and the fit looks good. It would look good for any two wandering series, related or not. That is the spurious regression, noticed in the 1920s and shown by Monte Carlo in the 1970s: a confident t statistic between two series that have nothing to do with each other (Granger and Newbold 1974; Pesaran 2015, 26).

What rescues the regression is its residual. If prices and income share one trend, the gap between them does not wander: it is stationary, and the slope is a long-run elasticity. Two series that are each I(1), integrated of order one, stationary only after differencing, but have a stationary linear combination are cointegrated (Engle and Granger 1987; Pesaran 2015, 523). The test is to regress, take the residual, and test it for a unit root (Engle and Granger 1987; Pesaran 2015, 525). In one state over 29 years that test has almost no power: cointegration tests in a single unit are weak at short T (Baltagi 2021, 357), for the same reason the unit-root tests of the unit-root note are.

So the panel is pooled for power, and the proviso of that note returns: the states must bring independent evidence. The panel also adds a trap of its own. Stack N independent spurious regressions, and the pooled slope no longer wanders as a single spurious slope does: it converges to a long-run average, with a normal limit, whether or not any cointegration exists (Phillips and Moon 1999; Baltagi 2021, 355). Its t statistic still grows with \(\sqrt T\), so the tendency to find a relation that is not there “may be even stronger in panel data regressions than in the pure time series case” (Kao 1999; Pesaran 2015, 844). A sensible pooled slope is no evidence of a long-run relation. Only the residual is.

I.2 One unit: a regression and a rank

Write the cointegrating regression of state i as

\[y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i x_{it} + u_{it},\]

with \(y\) log price and \(x\) log income. The Engle–Granger test runs OLS, then a Dickey–Fuller regression on \(\hat u_{it}\) with no intercept and no trend; a rejection of the unit root is read as cointegration (Engle and Granger 1987; Pesaran 2015, 525). The critical values are not those of a unit-root test on an observed series. Under the null the regression is spurious, so the residual has a distribution of its own, tabulated for it (Phillips and Ouliaris 1990; Pesaran 2015, 525). The result also depends on which series is on the left, and the test can see at most one relation (Pesaran 2015, 525–26).

Johansen’s test removes both limits by treating the pair as a system. Stack \(z_{it} = (y_{it}, x_{it})'\), write its vector autoregression of order \(p\) in levels with coefficient matrices \(\Phi_{ij}\), and rewrite it in error correction form,

\[\Delta z_{it} = -\Pi_i z_{i,t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p-1} \Gamma_{ij} \Delta z_{i,t-j} + u_{it}, \qquad \Pi_i = I - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \Phi_{ij},\]

the form of Pesaran (2015, 529, eq. 22.17). If \(z_{it}\) is I(1), the left side and the lagged differences are stationary, so \(\Pi_i z_{i,t-1}\) must be too, and that forces \(\Pi_i\) to have reduced rank, \(\Pi_i = \alpha_i \beta_i'\) (Pesaran 2015, 529, eq. 22.18). The rank \(r_i\) of \(\Pi_i\) is the number of stationary combinations, and \(\beta_i\) holds them (Johansen 1988, 1991; Pesaran 2015, 529). With two series, \(r_i = 0\) means no shared trend, \(r_i = 1\) one shared trend, and \(r_i = 2\) that both series are stationary, which contradicts the unit-root result the chapter started from. The trace statistic for rank \(r\) against rank \(m\) is

\[\mathrm{LR}(H_r \mid H_m) = -T \sum_{i=r+1}^{m} \log(1 - \hat\lambda_i),\]

built from the smallest eigenvalues of the reduced-rank regression, and it does not change when the series are reordered (Johansen 1991; Pesaran 2015, 541). Its limit is a multivariate Dickey–Fuller distribution, with critical values that depend on the number of series and on whether the constant and trend are restricted to the long-run relation or left free (Pesaran 2015, 543). That choice is Johansen’s five cases (Johansen 1994; Pesaran 2015, 539); the step’s default, an unrestricted constant and no trend, is the third case, which allows a drift in the levels. The whole apparatus is asymptotic, and Monte Carlo evidence says it is reliable only when T is reasonably large and the system small (Pesaran 2015, 547). At \(T = 29\) per state, the step’s 49 ranks are a tally, not 49 verdicts.

I.3 The precondition

The panel tests below are residual tests. Pesaran (2015, 839) states the condition under which a residual test is the right tool: \(r_i = 1\) in every unit, with the variables split as \((y_{it}, x_{it}')\) and no cointegration among the regressors \(x_{it}\). A system test allows \(r_i > 1\) and needs no such split. The two also treat the residual’s serial correlation differently. Residual tests absorb it in a long-run variance, the variance a serially correlated series shows in its running sums, estimated without a model; system tests model it with the VAR’s lags (Pesaran 2015, 839). With one regressor the second half of the condition holds by default, and the first half is what the state-by-state ranks check.

The single-equation estimators of the long run share the condition. All regressors must be I(1) and not cointegrated among themselves, or the long-run variance matrix they divide by is singular; and the relation must be one that \(y_{it}\) truly enters, since it is normalised to a unit coefficient (Pesaran 2015, 852).

Why not one system for the whole panel? Stack every state’s pair into one vector and run Johansen on it, and cointegration within states and across them is covered at once, with a free error covariance for the dependence. That works when the stacked vector has fewer than about ten series and \(T > 100\) (Banerjee et al. 2004; Pesaran 2015, 839–40); the number of parameters grows with \(N^2\), so T must be far larger than N (Pesaran 2015, 855). At 98 series and \(T = 29\) it is out of reach. In practice the dependence is carried by common factors, a few unobserved series that load on every unit, each to its own degree (common factors and CCE). That is the setting of equation 31.38 in Pesaran (2015, 840), and it is where the substitute below comes from.

I.4 Four panel tests

Kao. One slope for every unit, and the error tested for a unit root with the panel pooled. Take the within residuals \(\tilde e_{it}\), the residuals of the fit with a state intercept (fixed or random), and run

\[\tilde e_{it} = \rho \tilde e_{i,t-1} + v_{it}.\]

The null of no cointegration is \(\rho = 1\) (Kao 1999; Baltagi 2021, 357). Kao builds four Dickey–Fuller statistics from \(\hat\rho\) and its t ratio, two for regressors unrelated to the error at every date and two allowing the regressors and the error to be related, plus an ADF statistic from the lag-augmented regression. Each is shifted and scaled by a mean and variance and converges to \(N(0, 1)\) in sequential limits, T first and then N (Kao 1999; Baltagi 2021, 357–58; Pesaran 2015, 848). The correction assumes the nuisance parameters are the same for every unit (Pesaran 2015, 848).

McCoskey–Kao. The reverse null. With unit-specific slopes, the error is split into a random walk and a noise term, and the null of cointegration is that the random walk has zero variance (McCoskey and Kao 1998; Baltagi 2021, 359). The statistic is the LM form

\[\mathrm{LM} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{T^2} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{S_{it}^2}{\hat\sigma_e^2}, \qquad S_{it} = \sum_{j=1}^{t} \hat e_{ij},\]

a partial sum of residuals, and \(\sqrt N (\mathrm{LM} - \mu) \to N(0, \sigma^2)\) with moments found by simulation (McCoskey and Kao 1998; Baltagi 2021, 359). The residuals must come from an efficient estimator of the long run (Baltagi 2021, 359), and the test is the panel version of the single-series test of a stationary error (Pesaran 2015, 850). Two warnings come with it: the asymptotic approximation is often poor, and the test “is not equipped to deal with cross-sectional dependence” (Baltagi 2021, 359).

Pedroni. Unit-specific slopes, and seven statistics in two families (Pedroni 1999, 2004). The group-mean family computes a statistic unit by unit and averages, as a unit-root test against a heterogeneous alternative does; the panel family sums numerator and denominator over units before dividing, as a test against one common alternative does (Baltagi 2021, 360; Pesaran 2015, 848–49). Baltagi (2021, 360–61, eqs. 12.25–12.26) prints one of each. The group-mean \(\rho\) form is a sum of unit-by-unit Phillips–Ouliaris statistics (Phillips and Ouliaris 1990),

\[Z_{\tilde\rho} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\sum_{t}(\hat e_{i,t-1}\Delta\hat e_{it} - \hat\lambda_i)} {\sum_{t}\hat e_{i,t-1}^2},\]

with \(\hat\lambda_i\) half the gap between the long-run and the contemporaneous variance of unit i’s residual; the panel t form sums over units first, each unit weighted by its long-run conditional variance. Each statistic is read after centring and scaling by tabulated moments, \((Z - \mu\sqrt N)/\sigma \to N(0, 1)\) (Pedroni 1999; Pesaran 2015, 849); for the model with an intercept and no trend, the panel t form obeys \(Z_{\hat t} + 1.73\sqrt N \to N(0, 0.93)\) (Baltagi 2021, 361). Averaging pieces rather than whole statistics makes a rejection hard to read; the averaged statistics have the plain reading, that enough units sit far from what the null predicts (Baltagi 2021, 361).

Larsson–Lyhagen–Löthgren. The system test. Run Johansen’s trace test in each unit, centre and scale each statistic by its tabulated mean and variance, and average. The LR-bar statistic is IPS (Im et al. 2003), the mean of unit-by-unit statistics (first-generation tests), applied to trace statistics instead of ADF t ratios, and it is asymptotically \(N(0, 1)\) (Larsson et al. 2001; Baltagi 2021, 361; Pesaran 2015, 849–50). It tests no cointegration in any unit against at most \(r_0 \ge 1\) relations, and unlike the residual tests it allows more than one relation per unit; the moments are tabulated for the model without deterministic terms by Larsson et al. (2001) and with a constant and trend by Breitung (2005; Pesaran 2015, 850). It needs a large T: with a short T the size is severely distorted however large N is (Baltagi 2021, 361).

I.5 Dependence

The unit-root note ends on the assumption no test checks: units may share a trend, and the traps step shows that no correlation statistic sees it. For cointegration tests the consequence is stated outright. The literature assumes one cointegrating vector per unit and lets units depend on each other only through short-run dynamics; when units are cointegrated with each other, “both univariate and multivariate panel cointegration tests can be substantially over-sized” (Banerjee et al. 2004; Baltagi 2021, 362). The remedy proposed is to extract each unit’s common trend by Johansen’s method and test for cointegration among the trends (Baltagi 2021, 362), a procedure demonstrated on panels of at most eight units (Pesaran 2015, 843).

A common factor is the gentler case, and it is enough to break the tests. Under the factor structure of Bai and Ng (2004), Kao’s and Pedroni’s statistics are no longer asymptotically normal and converge at the rate \(T\) rather than \(\sqrt N T\); the remedy is to estimate the factors, strip them from the data, and test the defactored residuals (Gengenbach et al. 2006; Baltagi 2021, 362). On the unit-root side, PANIC, the test that defactors by principal components and tests factors and remainders separately, is invalid in its pooled form: replacing the remainders by estimates biases the pooled statistic, and Bai and Ng (2010) supply an alternative (Westerlund and Larsson 2009; Pesaran 2015, 837). Pesaran’s verdict on the field is that “a general approach that is capable of addressing all the various issues involved does not exist if N is relatively large” (Pesaran 2015, 843).

I.6 Long-run estimation

Once the residual is stationary, the slope is the long-run elasticity, and the question is how to estimate it. OLS on a cointegrated panel is consistent at rate \(T\sqrt N\), so the years do more than the states, but with regressors that respond to the error it is inefficient (Pesaran 2015, 850). Two corrections share the field.

FMOLS adjusts the dependent variable for the part of the error that is correlated with the regressor’s innovations and subtracts a bias term from the cross product, both built from non-parametric long-run variances (Phillips and Moon 1999; Pedroni 2000; Pesaran 2015, 850–51, eqs. 31.51–31.52). DOLS does the same by regression: add leads and lags of \(\Delta x_{it}\) to the cointegrating regression, so that what is left is orthogonal to the regressor’s past and future (Kao and Chiang 2000; Pesaran 2015, 851, eqs. 31.53–31.54). The two have the same limiting distribution,

\[T\sqrt N\,(\hat\beta - \beta) \to N\!\left(0,\; 6\,\Omega_\varepsilon^{-1} \sigma^2_{u \mid \varepsilon}\right)\]

in the homogeneous case (Kao and Chiang 2000; Pesaran 2015, 851). Both maintain cross-sectional independence (Baltagi 2021, 364). Both also rest on kernel estimates of long-run variances, which perform poorly when T is under 20 (Pesaran 2015, 851). Breitung’s two-step estimator, which fits the short-run and long-run parameters separately, has a smaller small-sample bias and more reliable standard errors than either in his experiments (Breitung 2005; Pesaran 2015, 853); in the large simulation of Wagner and Hlouskova (2010), DOLS beats every other estimator, with FMOLS and the two-step estimator similar (Pesaran 2015, 853).

Under dependence. Three estimators relax independence, and all three represent it as a correlation between the units’ errors at the same date. DSUR is a GLS fit of the DOLS regression, OLS after reweighting by the long-run error covariance across units, for one homogeneous cointegrating vector (Mark et al. 2005; Moon and Perron 2005; Pesaran 2015, 853–54, eq. 31.57). Bai–Kao and Westerlund add stationary common factors to the error and run FMOLS around them; Bai–Kao–Ng allow non-stationary factors and estimate slope, factors and loadings by iterating on a least-squares criterion, with a bias correction (Bai and Kao 2006; Westerlund 2007a; Bai et al. 2009; Pesaran 2015, 854, eqs. 31.58–31.59). None of them allows cross-unit cointegration (Pesaran 2015, 854). The panel VECM that would, Johansen on the stacked vector, is the infeasible system of the precondition section (Groen and Kleibergen 2003; Pesaran 2015, 854–55, eq. 31.60).

The substitute the book runs. None of the above is in R. What is in R is CCE, the regression that adds cross-state averages of price and income to each state’s equation to stand in for the common factors (CCE). Its estimates are consistent whether the factors are stationary, non-stationary or cointegrated, so long as the idiosyncratic error is stationary and the number of factors fixed (Kapetanios et al. 2011; Pesaran 2015, 845). On these 49 states Holly et al. (2010) report CCEMG and CCEP slopes of 1.14 (0.20) and 1.20 (0.21), do not reject a unit elasticity, and test the gap \(p_{it} - y_{it}\) with CIPS, the panel unit-root test that defactors with the same averages (second-generation tests): statistics of −2.16 to −2.45 against a 5% value of −2.11, read as cointegration “for a sufficiently large number of States” (Pesaran 2015, 845–46). Croissant and Millo (2019, 209) shorten the route and run CIPS directly on the CCE residuals, with no deterministic term because the fit already carries one, and conclude that both CCE fits “represent cointegrating regressions”.

The book runs the second version and labels it a substitute, for two reasons. The CIPS critical values are those of a unit-root test on an observed series; the single-series literature shows that a residual has a distribution of its own, which is why Engle–Granger has its own tables (Pesaran 2015, 525). Holly et al. (2010) avoid the problem by fixing the slope at one before testing; the residual version does not. And CIPS tests the alternative that a share of units has a stationary residual (Pesaran 2007) (what a rejection means), so a rejection says that enough states’ gaps revert, not that every state’s does. What the substitute keeps is the one thing the estimators above lack: it allows the common shock that the CD test, the test of whether units move together, found in every state.

I.7 Finite samples

Two Monte Carlo studies set expectations for \(T = 29\). Gutierrez (2003) finds that at \(T = 10\) Kao’s tests have more power than Pedroni’s, and still little even at \(N = 100\); at large T Pedroni’s win; both beat the LR-bar. The danger runs both ways: with a long panel the whole panel is called cointegrated when a small fraction of units is, and with a short one it is called not cointegrated when a large fraction is (Gutierrez 2003; Baltagi 2021, 363). Wagner and Hlouskova (2010) cover the single-equation and the system tests at once. Two of Pedroni’s statistics, the ADF-based ones, perform best; every other test is severely undersized with low power, “dismal for \(T \le 25\)”; Pedroni’s are also the least affected by short-run dependence or cross-unit cointegration; and the system tests are very bad at small T and unreliable for the rank (Wagner and Hlouskova 2010; Baltagi 2021, 363; Pesaran 2015, 849).

The project’s own check, in the Pitfalls of the cointegration step, is of the implementation rather than the tests. At \(N = 49\), \(T = 29\), the five scale-free statistics as computed there reject in almost none of 200 draws without cointegration and in all 200 with it, and the other two change with the units of measurement, which Pedroni’s statistics cannot. They separate the hypotheses and fail the \(N(0, 1)\) calibration, so the step reports them as unverified.

I.9 Open

  • No implementation. Kao, McCoskey–Kao, Larsson–Lyhagen–Löthgren, Westerlund’s error-correction tests (Westerlund 2007b), FMOLS, DOLS, DSUR and the factor estimators have no R implementation in this project. The Stata benchmarks, xtcointtest kao, xtcointtest pedroni and xtcointtest westerlund, are deferred to a later plan.
  • pco. The only Pedroni implementation is archived on CRAN, two of its seven statistics depend on the units of measurement, and the moments it standardises with cannot be checked against Table 2 of Pedroni (1999), which is not in lit/. Its five remaining statistics are reported unverified.
  • The precondition at \(T = 29\). \(r_i = 1\) cannot be confirmed state by state; the tally of ranks is the evidence.
  • The substitute’s critical values. CIPS on a regression residual reads unit-root tables. No panel residual-based table exists for it, and this book does not simulate one.
  • Baltagi’s targets. Tables 12.3 to 12.7 of Baltagi (2021) run Kao, Pedroni, FMOLS and DOLS on the R&D panel of Kao et al. (1999), which ships in no R package. They are not reproduced.
