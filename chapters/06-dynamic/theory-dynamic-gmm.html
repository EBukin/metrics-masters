Appendix L — Dynamic panel GMM

Why a lagged outcome breaks fixed effects and how instruments fix it

Code: the bias, difference and instrument, difference and system GMM and diagnostics.

L.1 Why the within estimator breaks

A firm does not jump to the workforce it wants. Hiring takes months and firing costs money, so this year’s employment is last year’s employment moved part of the way toward a target set by wages, capital and demand. Put last year’s outcome on the right-hand side and the panel regression is dynamic:

\[ y_{it} = \gamma\, y_{i,t-1} + x_{it}'\beta + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, N;\ t = 2, \dots, T, \]

with \(\alpha_i\) the firm effect and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) an innovation that is serially uncorrelated and uncorrelated with \(\alpha_i\) (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 8.1–8.2; Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 7.1). The coefficient γ is the persistence of the outcome: how much of last year survives into this year. The 140 UK firms of Arellano and Bond (1991), observed 1976–1984, are the panel on which every method in this note was first run, and the benchmark data set of Stata and EViews (Baltagi 2021, 193; Croissant and Millo 2019, 183).

Two things go wrong at once. The effect \(\alpha_i\) is in every period’s \(y\), so it is in \(y_{i,t-1}\) too: the lagged outcome is correlated with the error, and pooled OLS is biased upward, by an amount that vanishes only when \(\sigma^2_\alpha\) does (Baltagi 2021, 187; Croissant and Millo 2019, 163–64). The obvious cure is the within transformation of the core chapter, which subtracts each firm’s mean and so removes \(\alpha_i\). It trades one correlation for another. The demeaned lag \(y_{i,t-1} - \bar y_{i,-1}\) contains every period’s \(y\) and hence every period’s \(\varepsilon\); the demeaned error \(\varepsilon_{it} - \bar\varepsilon_i\) contains every period’s \(\varepsilon\) as well, among them the \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) that sits inside \(y_{i,t-1}\). The two share a term in every period, and the sum of those shared terms is the Nickell bias: of order \(1/T\), so it does not shrink as more firms are added, only as more years are (Nickell 1981; Baltagi 2021, 188; Croissant and Millo 2019, 165). It is a bias of demeaning, not of the fixed-effects assumption: random-effects GLS quasi-demeans and is biased the same way (Pesaran 2015, 678).

The size of it is known exactly. For the pure autoregression with no regressors, Pesaran (2015, eqs. 27.7–27.11) takes the large-\(N\) limit of the within estimator from the moments of the demeaned lag. In the form Croissant and Millo (2019, 165) print, with \(T\) the number of periods in the regression once one lag is taken and \(A = 1 - (1 - \gamma^{T}) / \{T(1 - \gamma)\}\),

\[ \operatorname*{plim}_{N \to \infty}\,(\hat\gamma_{FE} - \gamma) = -\,\frac{1 + \gamma}{T - 1}\; \frac{A}{1 - \dfrac{2\gamma A}{(1 - \gamma)(T - 1)}} . \]

Compactly, the limit is \(-(1 + \gamma)/T + O(T^{-2})\): negative for \(\gamma \geq 0\), equal to \(-1/T\) at \(\gamma = 0\), and large when \(T\) is in the 3 to 8 range typical of household and firm panels (Pesaran 2015, 679–80). At \(\gamma = 0.5\) and \(T = 10\), which CM call “a fairly long time span”, the bias is \(-0.167\): a third of the coefficient (Croissant and Millo 2019, 165). Longer panels do not make it safe. In the Monte Carlo of Judson and Owen (1999), with \(N = 20\) or \(100\) and \(T\) up to 30, the bias grows with γ, falls with \(T\), and even at \(T = 30\) can still be 20 % of the true value (Baltagi 2021, 188). The within estimator is consistent only as \(T \to \infty\) (Baltagi 2021, 188).

Two numbers bracket the truth. OLS is biased up, within is biased down, so any consistent estimator should land between them (Croissant and Millo 2019, 168). The bias step draws exactly that picture. Three ways out exist: correct the within estimator for its known bias (Kiviet 1995; Baltagi 2021, 188), write a likelihood that handles the initial observation (Hsiao et al. 2002; Pesaran 2015, sec. 27.6), or remove \(\alpha_i\) by a transformation that leaves a usable instrument behind. This note is about the third.

L.2 The minimum math

Difference, then instrument

First differences remove the effect without averaging over time,

\[ \Delta y_{it} = \gamma\, \Delta y_{i,t-1} + \Delta x_{it}'\beta + \Delta\varepsilon_{it}, \qquad t = 3, \dots, T, \]

and with it every time-invariant regressor (Baltagi 2021, eq. 8.4 and p. 192; Pesaran 2015, eq. 27.19). The lag is still endogenous: \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\) contains \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it} = \varepsilon_{it} - \varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) contains it with a minus sign, so \(E[\Delta y_{i,t-1}\Delta\varepsilon_{it}] = -\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) and OLS on the differenced equation is inconsistent (Pesaran 2015, 682). The differenced error is also a first-order moving average: its variance is \(2\sigma^2_\varepsilon\), its first autocovariance \(-\sigma^2_\varepsilon\), and all higher ones zero (Pesaran 2015, 682; Croissant and Millo 2019, 166).

An instrument is a variable correlated with the regressor that causes trouble and uncorrelated with the error. Anderson and Hsiao (1982) saw that \(y_{i,t-2}\) is one: it is correlated with \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\), and it is uncorrelated with \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\) as long as the innovations themselves are serially uncorrelated. The difference \(\Delta y_{i,t-2}\) qualifies on the same argument (Baltagi 2021, 188; Pesaran 2015, 682). Between the two, Arellano (1989) found that the estimator on the differenced instrument has a singularity point and very large variances over a significant range of parameter values, while the one on the level has no singularity and much smaller variances, and is the one to use (Baltagi 2021, 188; Croissant and Millo 2019, 167). The within transformation cannot take the place of differencing here: its error \(\varepsilon_{it} - \bar\varepsilon_i\) contains every realisation of \(\varepsilon\), so no lag of \(y\) is uncorrelated with it (Croissant and Millo 2019, 166). (Orthogonal deviations, each observation minus the mean of those after it, are the other transformation that works, and lose one observation per gap instead of two (Croissant and Millo 2019, 166–67).)

The instrument is not strong. With no regressors, \(T = 3\), and the innovations uncorrelated with \(\alpha_i\) and with \(y_{i0}\), the level instrument has

\[ E[y_{i,t-2}\,\Delta y_{i,t-1}] = -\sigma^2_\varepsilon\,(1 - \gamma)\, \frac{1 - \gamma^{2(t-1)}}{1 - \gamma^2}, \]

non-zero for \(|\gamma| < 1\) but shrinking toward zero as γ approaches one, and the approach breaks down at \(\gamma = 1\) (Pesaran 2015, 682). Anderson–Hsiao is consistent but not efficient, for two reasons: it uses one instrument where several are valid, and it ignores the moving-average structure of the differenced error (Baltagi 2021, 188; Pesaran 2015, 682; Croissant and Millo 2019, 168).

Every lag is an instrument

Arellano and Bond (1991) fixed both. In the first usable period, \(t = 3\), the equation \(\Delta y_{i3} = \gamma \Delta y_{i2} + \Delta\varepsilon_{i3}\) has one valid instrument, \(y_{i1}\). At \(t = 4\) both \(y_{i1}\) and \(y_{i2}\) are uncorrelated with \(\Delta\varepsilon_{i4}\); one more instrument arrives with each period, until at \(t = T\) the set is \(y_{i1}, \dots, y_{i,T-2}\) (Baltagi 2021, 189–90; Pesaran 2015, 683; Croissant and Millo 2019, 168). Stacked, the instruments of one firm form the block-diagonal matrix

\[ W_i = \begin{pmatrix} [y_{i1}] & & & 0 \\ & [y_{i1}, y_{i2}] & & \\ & & \ddots & \\ 0 & & & [y_{i1}, \dots, y_{i,T-2}] \end{pmatrix}, \]

one row per differenced period, and the claim that each block is uncorrelated with its period’s error is the set of moment conditions \(E(W_i'\Delta\varepsilon_i) = 0\) (Baltagi 2021, eq. 8.6; Pesaran 2015, eqs. 27.27–27.28; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 7.4). Holtz-Eakin et al. (1988) and Ahn and Schmidt (1995) stated the same conditions (Baltagi 2021, 190). Their number is \(\tfrac12(T-1)(T-2)\) for \(T\) periods: 36 at \(T = 10\), and it grows with the square of \(T\) (Croissant and Millo 2019, 172).

The generalised method of moments (GMM) turns the conditions into an estimator. Replace the expectation by the sample mean \(m(\theta) = N^{-1}\sum_i W_i'\Delta\varepsilon_i(\theta)\), and since there are more conditions than parameters, choose θ to make \(m(\theta)'\,A\,m(\theta)\) as small as possible for a weighting matrix \(A\) (Hansen 1982; Croissant and Millo 2019, eqs. 7.5–7.7). The efficient weight is the inverse of the covariance of the moments (Croissant and Millo 2019, 169). If the innovations are homoskedastic and serially uncorrelated, that covariance is known up to scale, because the differenced error of a firm has \(E(\Delta\varepsilon_i\Delta\varepsilon_i') = \sigma^2_\varepsilon G\) with \(G\) the \((T-2) \times (T-2)\) band matrix of 2 on the diagonal and \(-1\) beside it (Baltagi 2021, eq. 8.5; Pesaran 2015, eq. 27.33; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 7.8). Using \(A = (\sum_i W_i' G W_i)^{-1}\) gives the one-step estimator,

\[ \hat\gamma_1 = \big[\Delta y_{-1}' W (W'(I_N \otimes G)W)^{-1} W' \Delta y_{-1}\big]^{-1} \big[\Delta y_{-1}' W (W'(I_N \otimes G)W)^{-1} W' \Delta y\big], \]

(Baltagi 2021, eq. 8.8). The two-step estimator replaces the assumed covariance by one built from the one-step residuals, \(V_N = \sum_i W_i'\,\Delta\hat\varepsilon_i\Delta\hat\varepsilon_i'\,W_i\),

\[ \hat\gamma_2 = \big[\Delta y_{-1}' W V_N^{-1} W' \Delta y_{-1}\big]^{-1} \big[\Delta y_{-1}' W V_N^{-1} W' \Delta y\big], \qquad \widehat{\operatorname{var}}(\hat\gamma_2) = \big[\Delta y_{-1}' W V_N^{-1} W' \Delta y_{-1}\big]^{-1}, \]

which is the optimal GMM estimator of Hansen (1982) for \(N \to \infty\) at fixed \(T\), needs no assumption about the initial observations or the distributions of \(\alpha_i\) and \(\varepsilon_{it}\), and is robust to heteroskedasticity and serial correlation in the weight (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 8.9–8.10 and pp. 190–191; Croissant and Millo 2019, 171). The two steps are asymptotically equivalent when the innovations are IID (Baltagi 2021, 191). Regressors join on the same logic: a strictly exogenous \(x_{it}\) instruments itself, a predetermined one contributes its lags \(x_{i1}, \dots, x_{i,t-1}\) as further columns of \(W_i\) (Pesaran 2015, 683–85). This is difference GMM.

The system and its price

Difference GMM inherits the weakness of its instruments. Take the autoregression with \(T = 3\), where the single moment condition \(E(y_{i1}\Delta\varepsilon_{i3}) = 0\) just identifies γ and GMM is plain two-stage least squares. The first stage regresses \(\Delta y_{i2}\) on \(y_{i1}\), and the model says \(\Delta y_{i2} = (\gamma - 1)\,y_{i1} + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{i2}\), so with \(c = (1 - \gamma)/(1 + \gamma)\) the first-stage coefficient has

\[ \operatorname{plim}\,\hat\pi = (\gamma - 1)\, \frac{c}{c + \sigma^2_\alpha / \sigma^2_\varepsilon} , \]

which is pulled toward zero as γ approaches one or as the effect variance grows relative to the innovation variance; the first-stage \(F\) converges to a non-central \(\chi^2_1\) whose non-centrality goes to zero as \(\gamma \to 1\) (Blundell and Bond 1998; Baltagi 2021, eqs. 8.33–8.35; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 7.19). The series is nearly a unit root, the random walk of the time chapter, and lagged levels then say almost nothing about current changes. The estimator is biased and imprecise, and Blundell and Bond (1998) named the cause: weak instruments (Baltagi 2021, 201; Pesaran 2015, 685).

Arellano and Bover (1995) and Blundell and Bond (1998) add a second equation. Keep the model in levels, \(y_{it} = \gamma y_{i,t-1} + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it}\), and instrument \(y_{i,t-1}\) with the lagged difference \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\), which carries no \(\alpha_i\). The moment conditions

\[ E\big[\Delta y_{i,t-1}\,(\alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it})\big] = 0, \qquad t = 3, \dots, T, \]

are \(T - 2\) in number and not redundant given the difference conditions (Baltagi 2021, 202); the longer lags \(\Delta y_{i,t-s}\), \(s \geq 2\), would add nothing, since each is a combination of conditions already used (Croissant and Millo 2019, 175–76). Stacking the two sets of equations and the two sets of instruments gives system GMM, which also estimates an intercept (Croissant and Millo 2019, 177). Its price is an assumption about where each firm started. The levels conditions hold if

\[ E\Big[\Big(y_{i1} - \frac{\alpha_i}{1 - \gamma}\Big)\alpha_i\Big] = 0 : \]

the deviation of the first observation from the firm’s long-run mean is uncorrelated with the effect, so that from the second period on each series converges toward its own mean (Baltagi 2021, eq. 8.36; Pesaran 2015, eqs. 27.37–27.38). Croissant and Millo (2019, 176) show that this initial-condition restriction either holds in every period or in none, and read it pragmatically: the deviation decays at rate γ, so a process that started long ago is near its steady state and the condition is close enough. Its validity is what the whole estimator rests on (Pesaran 2015, 689), which is why the Sargan test of the next section is best run on the levels conditions alone as well as on the full set (Croissant and Millo 2019, 180).

What the extra equation buys is large when the instruments of the difference equation are weak. For \(T = 4\) and \(\sigma^2_\alpha / \sigma^2_\varepsilon = 1\), the asymptotic variance of difference GMM divided by that of system GMM is 1.75 at \(\gamma = 0\), 3.26 at \(\gamma = 0.5\) and 55.4 at \(\gamma = 0.9\) (Blundell and Bond 1998; Baltagi 2021, 202); the Monte Carlo of Blundell et al. (2000) finds the same in finite samples, especially in the weak-instrument case (Pesaran 2015, 689). On a production function for 509 US firms, difference GMM gave a low capital coefficient with low precision and system GMM a reasonable one that did not reject constant returns (Blundell and Bond 2000; Baltagi 2021, 202). Two cautions. Bun and Windmeijer (2010) show that system GMM keeps a weak-instrument problem of its own: its bias and root mean squared error are smaller than difference GMM’s when the series is persistent, but both grow with \(\sigma^2_\alpha / \sigma^2_\varepsilon\) and can become substantial, and its Wald test, already size-distorted for both estimators with persistent data, deteriorates further as that ratio rises (Baltagi 2021, 202). And the weakness itself depends on the initial condition: Hayakawa (2009) shows that when the initial condition leaves the series’ mean non-stationary and the effect variance is much larger than the innovation variance, difference GMM has no weak-instrument problem at all (Baltagi 2021, 202).

L.3 Reading the diagnostics

Overidentification. With more moment conditions than parameters, the conditions GMM did not need to fit can be checked. The statistic of Sargan (1958), applied to GMM by Hansen (1982), is the minimised objective of the two-step estimator,

\[ S = \Delta\hat\varepsilon' W \Big(\sum_i W_i' \Delta\hat\varepsilon_i\Delta\hat\varepsilon_i' W_i\Big)^{-1} W' \Delta\hat\varepsilon \;\sim\; \chi^2_{J - K}, \]

with \(J\) the number of instrument columns and \(K\) the number of coefficients, under the null that every moment condition holds (Baltagi 2021, 191; Pesaran 2015, 691; Croissant and Millo 2019, 180). A rejection says at least one instrument is correlated with the error. A non-rejection says less than it seems. Bowsher (2002) fixed \(N = 100\) and let \(T\) run from 5 to 15 with the full Arellano–Bond instrument set. At \(T = 5\) the test behaves: size 0.052 at the 5 % level, power 0.742. At \(T = 15\) the \(\chi^2_{90}\) statistic has Monte Carlo mean 91.3, close to its theoretical 90, but Monte Carlo variance 13.7 against a theoretical 180, and the rejection rate is zero under the null and under the alternative alike. The cause is a poorly estimated weighting matrix, not weak instruments (Baltagi 2021, 192; Pesaran 2015, 691). In applied work the symptom is a \(p\)-value near one: Roodman (2009a) re-ran two published growth regressions, and in one of them the test’s \(p\)-value of 0.97 fell to 0.001 once the instrument set was cut (Croissant and Millo 2019, 180–83).

Serial correlation. Everything above assumed the innovations are serially uncorrelated, the property tested on static residuals in the core chapter. Here it is the condition that makes \(y_{i,t-2}\) a valid instrument: consistency rests on \(E[\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\Delta\varepsilon_{i,t-2}] = 0\) (Baltagi 2021, 192; Pesaran 2015, 685). Arellano and Bond (1991) test it on the differenced residuals with the statistic

\[ m_l = \frac{a_l}{\sqrt{\hat b_l}}, \qquad a_l = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\,\Delta\hat\varepsilon'\,\Delta\hat\varepsilon_{-l}, \]

where \(\Delta\hat\varepsilon_{-l}\) is the residual lagged \(l\) periods and \(\hat b_l\) a variance that accounts for the estimated coefficients; \(m_l\) is standard normal under no serial correlation of order \(l\) (Croissant and Millo 2019, 181–82). The two orders read differently. Order one must reject, because \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) share \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) by construction. Order two must not: \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon_{i,t-2}\) are correlated only if \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) and \(\varepsilon_{i,t-2}\) are, that is, if the innovations in levels are serially correlated of order one, and then the instruments dated \(t - 2\) fail (Baltagi 2021, 192; Croissant and Millo 2019, 182). The null of \(m_2\) is also true if the innovations follow a random walk, in which case OLS on the differenced model is consistent as well and Arellano and Bond (1991) give a Hausman-type test, a comparison of the two estimates, to tell the cases apart (Baltagi 2021, 192).

Two-step standard errors. The first Monte Carlo on the estimator, 100 replications with \(N = 100\) and \(T = 7\), found GMM nearly unbiased and much more precise than Anderson–Hsiao, and its two-step standard errors too small (Arellano and Bond 1991; Baltagi 2021, 193). Windmeijer (2005) traced the shortfall to the weighting matrix: \(V_N\) is built from one-step residuals, so the two-step estimator is a nonlinear function of the data through them, and the usual formula ignores that. A first-order expansion of the estimator in the estimated weight gives the Windmeijer correction, which adds to the conventional two-step covariance \(V_2\) the terms \(V_2 D' + D V_2 + D V_1 D'\), with \(D\) the gradient of the estimator with respect to the weight and \(V_1\) the one-step covariance (Croissant and Millo 2019, eqs. 7.27–7.29). The correction is accurate when all the moment conditions are linear, as they are here; without it, Wald tests on two-step estimates are oversized (Baltagi 2021, 193). The imprecision is structural: the moment covariance has \(J(J+1)/2\) free elements, each a fourth-order moment of the data, estimated from \(N\) firms (Croissant and Millo 2019, 178). Bond et al. (2001) go one step further and test hypotheses by the difference of two Sargan statistics instead of a Wald statistic, with better size in their Monte Carlo (Baltagi 2021, 193).

Too many instruments. The count \(\tfrac12(T-1)(T-2)\) is only for the lags of \(y\); with \(T = 10\) and five strictly exogenous regressors it reaches 500 (Baltagi 2021, 194). Ziliak (1997) asked whether the bias–efficiency trade-off known from time series survives in panels with hundreds of units, and found that it does: on 532 men over ten years, the GMM estimate of the intertemporal substitution elasticity of labour supply moved from 0.519 to 0.093 as the moment conditions went from 9 to 212, while its standard error fell from 0.36 to 0.07. He attributed the downward bias to the correlation between the sample moments and the estimated weighting matrix, and recommended a sub-optimal instrument set over the full one (Baltagi 2021, 194). The mechanism is overfitting: the more instruments, the closer the first-stage fit, until with as many instruments as observations the instrumental-variables estimator is OLS (Croissant and Millo 2019, 172; Roodman 2009b). Two cuts are standard. Limit the lag depth, which at \(T = 10\) and three lags gives 21 instruments instead of 36 (Croissant and Millo 2019, 172–73). Or collapse the matrix, keeping one column per lag distance instead of one per lag and period, so that the \(T - 2\) moments \(\sum_t y_{i,t-2}\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\), \(\sum_t y_{i,t-3}\Delta\varepsilon_{it}, \dots\) replace the full block diagonal (Roodman 2009a; Croissant and Millo 2019, eq. 7.18; Baltagi 2021, 194). A result that holds across lag depths and under collapsing is the result; one that moves with the instrument count, as Ziliak’s did, is not. Large \(T\) brings its own version of the problem: as \(T \to \infty\) the estimator stays asymptotically normal but carries a bias of order \(1/N\) unless \(T/N \to 0\) (Pesaran 2015, 685).

L.4 Four notations

The three anchor texts and this book each write the model differently, and the lag coefficient changes letter with each:

Where Lag coefficient Effect and innovation
Baltagi (2021, eq. 8.1) δ \(\mu_i + \nu_{it}\)
Pesaran (2015, eq. 27.1) λ \(\alpha_i + u_{it}\)
Croissant and Millo (2019, sec. 7.1) ρ \(\eta_n + \varepsilon_{nt}\)
this book γ \(\alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it}\)

Baltagi’s \(T\) counts the periods observed, Pesaran’s the periods in the regression after the initial observation, and this book follows the latter, so that the bias formula above is read with \(T\) equal to the number of years that enter the regression. The estimators keep their names across texts: difference GMM is Arellano–Bond, system GMM is Blundell–Bond, and pgmm calls the first transformation = "d" and the second "ld"; the steps own every other argument.

L.5 If / then

If Then
The model has a lagged outcome and \(T\) is short The within slope on the lag is biased down by about \((1 + \gamma)/T\); do not read it as the persistence (Pesaran 2015, 679; Baltagi 2021, 188)
\(T\) is 30 or less The bias can still be 20 % of γ; use an instrumental estimator (Judson and Owen 1999; Baltagi 2021, 188)
OLS and within disagree on the lag They should: OLS above, within below. A consistent estimate lands between them (Croissant and Millo 2019, 168)
One instrument is enough Use the level \(y_{i,t-2}\), not the difference \(\Delta y_{i,t-2}\) (Arellano 1989; Baltagi 2021, 188)
γ is near one or \(\sigma^2_\alpha\) dwarfs \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) Lagged levels are weak instruments for differences; difference GMM is biased and imprecise; consider system GMM (Baltagi 2021, eqs. 8.34–8.35; Pesaran 2015, 688)
System GMM is used The initial-condition restriction is assumed; test the levels moments on their own, and expect the bias to grow with the variance ratio (Pesaran 2015, 689; Croissant and Millo 2019, 180; Bun and Windmeijer 2010; Baltagi 2021, 202)
\(m_1\) rejects and \(m_2\) does not The pattern the estimator needs (Baltagi 2021, 192)
\(m_2\) rejects The innovations are serially correlated; the instruments dated \(t - 2\) are invalid (Baltagi 2021, 192; Croissant and Millo 2019, 182)
The Sargan \(p\)-value is near one with many instruments Not clean moments but the Bowsher pathology; cut the lags or collapse and test again (Baltagi 2021, 192; Croissant and Millo 2019, 180–81)
Two-step estimates are reported Report Windmeijer-corrected standard errors; the conventional ones are too small and the Wald tests oversized (Windmeijer 2005; Baltagi 2021, 193)
The instrument count approaches \(N\) The estimator drifts toward OLS; the coefficient that moves with the count is not a result (Croissant and Millo 2019, 172; Baltagi 2021, 194)

L.7 Open

  • Ahn–Schmidt moments. Under the standard assumptions there are \(T - 2\) further conditions, \(E(u_{iT}\Delta u_{it}) = 0\) for \(t = 2, \dots, T-1\), nonlinear in the parameters, and with homoskedastic innovations \(T - 1\) more; GMM on the full set is asymptotically efficient with the same asymptotic variance as maximum likelihood under normality (Ahn and Schmidt 1995; Baltagi 2021, eq. 8.26 and p. 201; Pesaran 2015, eq. 27.36). Not in plm; pdynmc runs them (Fritsch et al. 2021), and the GMM step shows the fit in its pitfalls box.
  • Bias-corrected within. Kiviet (1995) approximates the within bias with exogenous regressors and subtracts a consistent estimate of it (Baltagi 2021, 188; Pesaran 2015, 680); balanced panels only, and no endogenous regressors (Croissant and Millo 2019, 166). Not in R. Benchmark: Stata xtlsdvc, deferred.
  • Transformed likelihood. Hsiao et al. (2002) difference the model, give the first difference \(\Delta y_{i1}\) its own mean and variance, and maximise a likelihood in a fixed number of parameters; in their Monte Carlo it dominates GMM in small samples, at the price of cross-sectionally homoskedastic innovations (Pesaran 2015, sec. 27.6, pp. 692–695). Not in R. Benchmark: Stata xtdpdqml (Kripfganz 2016; Baltagi 2021, 197), deferred.
  • Time-invariant regressors. Differencing removes them. Arellano and Bover (1995) recover them inside a Hausman–Taylor (Hausman and Taylor 1981) system, a random-effects IV estimator that uses the exogenous regressors as instruments, with a block-diagonal instrument matrix (Baltagi 2021, sec. 8.3, pp. 194–198; Pesaran 2015, sec. 27.4.4, pp. 686–687); Kripfganz and Schwarz (2019) instead estimate the time-varying coefficients by GMM or quasi-maximum likelihood, regress the first-step residuals on the time-invariant regressors, and correct the second-step standard errors (Baltagi 2021, 197–98). Not in R. Benchmark: Stata xtseqreg (Baltagi 2021, 197), deferred.
  • Forward filtering. The Keane–Runkle estimator removes a general serial correlation pattern by a Cholesky transform before two-stage least squares with predetermined instruments (Keane and Runkle 1992; Baltagi 2021, sec. 8.6, p. 203; Pesaran 2015, sec. 27.5, pp. 691–692). Named only.
