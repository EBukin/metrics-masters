Appendix A — Cross-sectional dependence: how much, and of what kind

The exponent α and the unordered tests

Code: the exponent α for α and the routing, testing for CSD for the CD family. Spatial-structure tests are testing for spatial dependence.

A.1 The two questions people confuse

Units move together. Almost always, in almost every panel. So “is there cross-sectional dependence?” is nearly never the useful question — with a large N, even an average pairwise correlation of 0.02 will reject.

The two questions that actually matter are different:

  1. Is the co-movement pervasive? — does it involve a growing share of units, or a sparse, local handful? → this is α.
  2. Does it have geographic structure? — is unit i correlated with its neighbours specifically? → this is the ordered family, testing for spatial dependence.

α answers the first. Everything in this note either measures it or is a coarse test whose validity is defined in terms of it.

A.2 1. What α counts

Take a factor model where only some units load on the common shock:

\[z_{it} = a_i + \gamma_i f_t + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad \#\{i : \gamma_i \neq 0\} = O(N^{\alpha}).\]

α is the growth rate of the number of connected units. That is the whole idea; everything else is consequence.

α Units sharing the shock Picture
1 a fixed fraction of N an oil price shock — everyone feels it
3/4 grows, but the share vanishes a regional bloc that widens slower than the panel
1/2 about \(\sqrt{N}\) sparse, local — a spatial weight matrix with bounded row sums

Averaging is how you see it. For the cross-section average \(\bar z_t\) (Bailey et al. 2016; Pesaran 2015b, eq. 29.6, p. 754):

\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar z_t) = \underbrace{\kappa_0 N^{2(\alpha-1)}}_{\text{co-movement}} + \underbrace{\kappa_1 N^{-1}}_{\text{what independence gives}} + O(N^{\alpha-2}), \qquad \tfrac12 < \alpha \le 1.\]

The second term is the textbook \(\sigma^2/N\). The first is the excess over it. Two readings fall straight out:

  • α = 1 → \(N^{0}\) → the excess never shrinks. Pooling more units never removes the common movement. This is strong dependence: common factors (Pesaran 2015b, 754).
  • α < 1 → the excess vanishes as \(N \to \infty\). Formally weak dependence, which is where essentially all spatial models live (Pesaran 2015b, 756, 802).
Note

Why the range is 1/2 < α ≤ 1. Below 1/2 the \(\kappa_1 N^{-1}\) term dominates the \(\kappa_0 N^{2(\alpha-1)}\) term, and α is no longer identified from the variance of the average — sparse dependence is simply indistinguishable from independence at the aggregate level. The restriction is not a technicality; it says there is nothing left to measure down there.

A.3 2. Reading α: the effective sample size

The label “weak” is asymptotic and can badly mislead in a finite panel. Set \(\kappa_0 N^{2(\alpha-1)} = \sigma^2/N_{\text{eff}}\) and you get the number that actually matters — how many independent observations your N units are worth:

\[N_{\text{eff}} \;\propto\; N^{2(1-\alpha)}\]

α N = 100 N = 1 000 N = 9 000
1.00 1 1 1
0.90 2.5 4.0 6.2
0.75 10 32 95
0.50 100 1 000 9 000

Read the α = 0.90 row slowly. Nine thousand individuals carry about as much cross-sectional information as six independent ones, and going from 1 000 to 9 000 units bought you two. Yet α = 0.90 is formally “weak dependence”, and a great many published panels sit near it — Bailey et al. (2016) find most macroeconomic series in roughly the 0.77–0.92 range, with equity returns essentially at 1.

So: the weak/strong dichotomy is a limit theorem. α is the finite-sample truth. That gap is the entire reason to estimate it.

A.4 3. What α does not tell you

This is the easiest thing to get backwards, so state it plainly.

α says nothing about whether you need fixed effects. FE absorbs the time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity \(a_i\), and you need it for exactly the reasons you always did. What α governs is a different omitted variable: the time-varying common factor \(f_t\) with heterogeneous loadings \(\gamma_i\).

The identity that makes this click:

Two-way fixed effects is already a factor model — with \(\gamma_i = 1\) for everyone. A common time effect \(\delta_t\) absorbs a common shock only if every unit responds by the same amount.

So heterogeneous loadings are precisely the case two-way FE cannot remove, and α ≈ 1 is precisely where they bite. The correct summary:

Important

α does not tell you whether you need FE. It tells you whether FE is enough.

  • Low α → FE is sufficient. The omitted common shock, such as it is, washes out. Fix the standard errors and move on.
  • High α → FE is insufficient. \(f_t\) survives the within transformation, and if it correlates with your regressors the estimator is biased, not merely inefficient. No standard-error correction rescues a biased point estimate — not Driscoll–Kraay, not clustering, not spatial HAC. You need CCE (Pesaran 2006), explained in common factors and CCE, or interactive fixed effects.

A.5 4. The CD test

Pesaran’s CD (Pesaran 2004; Pesaran 2015b, eq. 29.76, p. 786) is the average pairwise residual correlation, scaled:

\[CD = \sqrt{\frac{2T}{N(N-1)}} \sum_{i<j} \hat\rho_{ij} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N(0,1).\]

Two design choices explain everything about its behaviour.

It sums raw \(\hat\rho\), not \(\hat\rho^{\,2}\). The Breusch–Pagan LM statistic (Breusch and Pagan 1980; Baltagi 2021, 412) sums \(T\hat\rho_{ij}^{\,2}\) into a \(\chi^2\), which needs \(T \gg N\) — with large N you are accumulating \(N(N-1)/2\) strictly positive terms and it diverges. CD is exactly centred at zero under the null for any T, because \(E(\hat\rho_{ij}) = 0\) in finite samples and not merely asymptotically. That is its virtue: small T, enormous N, still valid.

Signed summation is also its fatal flaw, in two specific ways:

  • Sign cancellation. Dependence whose pairwise correlations average to zero is invisible, however strong each individual pair. Positive and negative spatial weights are the obvious case.
  • Demeaned residuals are dead on arrival. Two-way FE forces \(\sum_i \hat e_{it} = 0\), which drives \(\bar\rho\) to roughly \(-1/(N-1) \approx 0\) mechanically. Running global CD on two-way FE residuals and reading non-rejection as “no dependence” is an artefact of the transformation, not evidence (Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 10.1.2.2).

The general statement of that second point is Juodis and Reese (2022): with period-specific parameters estimated rather than known, CD diverges as T grows even when the dependence has been correctly accounted for. It is an incidental parameters problem, and it applies to CCE residuals as much as to two-way FE residuals. This is the trap in §8’s defactor-then-re-diagnose sequence: the obvious diagnostic at step 3 is exactly the one that is invalid there. On US house prices, two-way FE moves \(\overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\) only from 0.51 to 0.49 while collapsing CD from 53.3 to 0.02, p = 0.98 — see testing for CSD.

And its null is not independence. The implicit null of CD is weak dependence — α < 1/4 when N and T grow at the same rate (Pesaran 2015a; 2015b, 786). Confirmed from the other direction at (Pesaran 2015b, 839), where CD is found to be the statistic most robust to spatial alternatives — which is to say, it does not fire on them.

Warning

CD is a one-bit read on α, and a coarse one. Its size is only assured below α ≈ 1/4; the statistic starts diverging somewhere above α ≈ 1/2. In between there is a band where it is neither reliably valid nor reliably powerful. Do not read a CD rejection as “there is dependence”; read it as “there is pervasive dependence”.

A.6 5. The LM family: which one, and when

All available through plm::pcdtest(test = ...).

test = Statistic Use it when Watch out
"cd" Pesaran CD, eq. 29.76 Large N, small T; the default first look Dies on demeaned residuals; blind to sign cancellation
"lm" Breusch–Pagan LM, eq. 13.32 (Baltagi 2021, 412) \(T \gg N\) only Diverges with large N; poorly centred at finite T
"sclm" Scaled LM Slightly larger N than "lm" tolerates Still badly centred at finite T: 14.25% rejection at N=200, T=20 against a nominal 5% (Pesaran 2015b, 789)
"bcsclm" Bias-corrected scaled LM, eq. 13.36 (Baltagi et al. 2012; Baltagi 2021, 414) The FE-appropriate one Requires a within model with individual or two-way effects
"rho", "absrho" \(\bar\rho\) and \(\overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\) Always — see §6 Not tests; no null, no p-value

If you are testing residuals from a fixed-effects model, "bcsclm" is the answer and the others are history. It exists precisely because the scaled LM’s finite-T bias, quantified above, makes it unusable at the N and T that panels actually have.

What plm does not have

Three later statistics attack CD’s blindness from different directions. None is in plm; all three are in dcce::pcd_test (Wasseja 2026).

test = Statistic What it changes Watch out
"cdw" Weighted CD (Juodis and Reese 2022) Rademacher weights, so estimated period-specific parameters cannot make CD diverge Buys size with power: it fails to reject even on raw series with \(\bar\rho = 0.29\). Seed-dependent
"pea" Power-enhanced (Fan et al. 2015) Adds a screening term that fires on sparse alternatives Enormous statistics; read it as a flag, not a magnitude
"cdstar" Bias-corrected CD* (Pesaran and Xie 2021) A PCA correction valid whether latent factors are weak or strong Needs the factor count n_pca; the default is 1

CD* is the one to reach for at step 3 of §8. It keeps power where CDw loses it and stays valid where plain CD does not.

A.7 6. ρ̄ versus |ρ̄|: the poor man’s α

The last row of that table is the most useful and the most ignored. "rho" gives you \(\bar\rho\); "absrho" gives you \(\overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\). Two numbers, no null hypothesis, and together they diagnose the exact failure mode of the test sitting next to them:

Pattern What it means What to do
\(\bar\rho \approx \overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\), both large Pervasive, same-signed co-movement You are near α = 1. Factor structure. Go to CCE.
\(\bar\rho \approx 0\), \(\overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\) large Real dependence that cancels in sign CD is blind here by construction. Go to the ordered tests.
both small Little dependence of any kind Low α. FE plus an honest vcov.

The middle row is the case where CD reports nothing and something is nonetheless badly wrong. Compute both; they cost nothing.

A.8 7. If / then

If Then
α ≈ 1, or \(\bar\rho \approx \overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\) and large Strong dependence. CCE (Pesaran 2006). FE is biased, not just inefficient.
α clearly below 1 and CD does not fire Weak dependence. FE is consistent — now ask whether it is spatial (testing for spatial dependence).
α in the 0.8–0.95 middle The modal case. Defactor first, then re-diagnose. See §8.
\(\bar\rho \approx 0\) but \(\overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\) large Sign cancellation. Ignore CD entirely; use the ordered tests.
You only have two-way FE residuals Global CD is uninformative by construction. Use "bcsclm", CD* (Pesaran and Xie 2021), or the randomised-W test (Millo 2017).
You have defactored (CCE, PC) residuals Same problem, general form (Juodis and Reese 2022). CD* or CDw, never plain CD.
Dependence confirmed, structure unknown Do not route on α alone. Corroborate with the rwtest vs pcdtest contrast (plan §5.7).

A.9 8. The middle is the normal case

Given that most real panels sit around α ≈ 0.8–0.95, the weak/strong fork is not usually a fork at all. It is a sequence, and Bailey et al. (2016) apply it that way — estimating α on the raw series and on residuals after removing factors:

  1. Estimate α (or read \(\bar\rho\) vs \(\overline{\lvert\rho\rvert}\)) on the raw series — csd_exp(use_residuals = TRUE), the Bailey et al. (2019) residual method. The Bailey et al. (2016) variable method, use_residuals = FALSE, only when the cross-section average carries the common trend; see §9.
  2. If it points strong → defactor (CCE, or principal components).
  3. Re-diagnose the residuals with csd_exp(use_residuals = TRUE), the Bailey et al. (2019) residual method, which is built for exactly this step. If α has dropped toward 1/2, the remaining dependence is local, and now modelling it spatially is legitimate. Do not use plain CD here — see Juodis and Reese (2022) in §4; use CD*.

Run on US house prices, the sequence gives α = 0.83 on the raw series and α = 0.51 on the CCEMG residuals, which is the clean version of this picture: the exponent α.

Fitting a spatial model to data with an unremoved common factor gets you a significant \(\hat\rho\) that is picking up the factor, not the geography. That is the single most common way this goes wrong.

A.10 9. Open

  • An implementation now exists, and it is young. dcce::csd_exp (Wasseja 2026) implements both the Bailey et al. (2016) variable method and the Bailey et al. (2019) residual method, and it reproduces its own vignette’s α = 0.8348 for pwt8 exactly. But dcce is a single-author package first published in 2026 with no independent validation, so the α numbers here rest on one cross-check: its Pesaran CD agrees with plm::pcdtest to four decimals on the same data. Corroborate α with the rwtest vs pcdtest contrast (plan §5.7) rather than routing on it alone.
  • The variable method is fragile in exactly the way the theory predicts. It inverts \(\hat\alpha = 1 + \ln(\hat\sigma^2_{\bar z}) / (2\ln N)\) with a correction for the \(\kappa_1 N^{-1}\) term, and that correction scales as \(\overline{\sigma^2_i} / (2 \ln N \cdot \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bar z_t))\). When the common component does not survive averaging, the correction dominates and the estimate leaves the parameter space entirely — below 1/2 on house prices, and large and negative on an independent panel. That is the callout in §1 made visible: there really is nothing to measure down there. The package does not constrain the output to (1/2, 1], so check the number against \(\bar\rho\) before believing it.
  • The 1/4-to-1/2 band. The size condition α < 1/4 (Pesaran 2015b, 786) and the point at which CD starts diverging are different quantities, and the gap between them is not well characterised here. Check against Pesaran (2015a) before relying on it.
