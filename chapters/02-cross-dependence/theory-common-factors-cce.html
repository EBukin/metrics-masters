Appendix B — Common factors and CCE: what cross-section averages are doing

Heterogeneous loadings, CCEMG and CCEP

Code: common correlated effects for the estimates, the exponent α for the routing that sends a panel here. Related note: cross-sectional dependence.

B.1 Why the method exists

Regress log house prices on log income state by state and average the 49 slopes: 0.30. Add two cross-state averages to each regression and do it again: 1.14 (Holly et al. 2010; Croissant and Millo 2019, 197–99). Nothing about the data changed. What changed is that the second regression controls for a national force, interest rates and credit conditions, that moves both prices and incomes in every state, each to its own degree.

The obvious control is a year effect: a dummy for each year, which removes whatever is common to all states in that year. It fails here for one reason. A year effect removes the force only if every state feels it by the same amount. When states differ in how hard the force hits, the part of it that differs stays in the error, correlated with income, and the slope is biased. Not inefficient: biased, and no standard-error correction helps cross-sectional dependence. CCE is the fix for that case, and this note says why it works and when it does not.

B.2 1. The model, and why year effects fail

State i in year t follows

\[ y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i x_{it} + u_{it}, \qquad u_{it} = \lambda_i f_t + \varepsilon_{it}, \]

where \(f_t\) is the unobserved common factor, the national force, and \(\lambda_i\) is state i’s loading on it, how hard the force hits that state. The factor is also in the regressor:

\[ x_{it} = a_i + \gamma_i f_t + v_{it}. \]

Income and the error share \(f_t\), so each state’s OLS slope picks up the factor’s effect on prices and attributes it to income. That is the leak.

A year effect subtracts the cross-state mean from every variable in every year. In the error that leaves \((\lambda_i - \bar\lambda) f_t\): the factor survives in proportion to how far each state’s loading sits from the average. Two-way fixed effects is a factor model with every loading equal to one, and it removes the factor only in that case. With heterogeneous loadings the bias stays, and α near 1 is exactly the panel where loadings are heterogeneous enough to matter (Pesaran 2015, 754).

B.3 2. Why averages work

Average the price equation over the N states in year t:

\[ \bar y_t = \bar\alpha + \bar\beta \bar x_t + \bar\lambda f_t + \bar\varepsilon_t + o_p(1), \]

and the income equation likewise, \(\bar x_t = \bar a + \bar\gamma f_t + \bar v_t\). As N grows the idiosyncratic averages \(\bar\varepsilon_t\) and \(\bar v_t\) vanish, and the two averages are left as two linear functions of one unknown, \(f_t\). Solve for it:

\[ f_t \approx \frac{\bar y_t - \bar\alpha - \bar\beta \bar x_t}{\bar\lambda}. \]

The unobserved factor is, for large N, a known combination of the observed averages. So put the averages themselves into each state’s regression,

\[ y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i x_{it} + c_i \bar y_t + d_i \bar x_t + e_{it}, \]

and let each state choose its own \(c_i\) and \(d_i\). Whatever \(\lambda_i\) is, the pair \((c_i, d_i)\) can absorb \(\lambda_i f_t\), because \(f_t\) lies in the space the averages span. The factor is controlled for without being estimated, and without assuming anything about its time path. That is the whole of Pesaran (2006), and it survives factors that wander like random walks (Kapetanios et al. 2011), which is why the method is safe on price levels.

With m factors and k regressors there are \(k + 1\) averages, and they span the factors only if \(m \le k + 1\) and the \((k+1) \times m\) matrix of average loadings has full column rank: the rank condition (Pesaran 2006). One regressor buys at most two factors.

B.4 3. CCEMG and CCEP

Two ways to turn 49 augmented regressions into one slope (Pesaran 2015, 767–68).

CCEMG, mean group, runs the augmented regression state by state and averages the slopes:

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{CCEMG}} = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N \hat\beta_i, \qquad \widehat{\operatorname{Var}} = \frac{1}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i=1}^N (\hat\beta_i - \hat\beta_{\text{CCEMG}})^2 . \]

The variance is the dispersion of the 49 slopes: no assumption about the errors within a state enters it, and it is honest about heterogeneous slopes, which is why the stripchart in the step is the estimate’s own picture.

CCEP, pooled, imposes one slope on every state while keeping the state-specific \(\alpha_i\), \(c_i\) and \(d_i\), and estimates it by least squares on the augmented data. Its variance is a sandwich that allows the true slopes to differ across states by random amounts (Croissant and Millo 2019, sec. 8.3.2.2). Under one common slope CCEP is the more efficient of the two. When the slopes differ, both estimate their mean, and CCEMG does so without weighting states by how much their income varies.

Read them together. CCEMG 1.14 and CCEP 1.20, each with a standard error of 0.2, are one number: the spread in the state slopes is noise around a common value, and the pooled estimate can be reported (Croissant and Millo 2019, 199). Had they been far apart, the slopes would be genuinely heterogeneous and CCEMG would be the one to report.

B.5 4. What CCE does not fix

  • Factors that average out. If the loadings on a factor average to zero across states, \(\bar\lambda = 0\), the averages do not carry it and the rank condition fails. Pesaran (2006) shows the estimators can survive this when the loadings are independent of the regressors’ loadings, but nothing in the output says whether that holds.
  • More factors than averages. With one regressor and three factors the averages span two of them. The remedy is more averages, of variables that load on the missing factors even if they are not in the regression, which plm::pcce does not provide. The exponent on the CCE residuals is the check: if it is still near 1, a factor was missed (the exponent α).
  • A lagged dependent variable. With \(y_{i,t-1}\) on the right, the augmented regression is biased at short T, and Chudik and Pesaran (2015) add lags of the averages to repair it. Dynamic panels are a later part of time dependence.
  • Weak dependence. When α is near ½ the averages carry nothing and CCE changes nothing. The dependence that remains is local, and a spatial model is the tool (does it follow the map).
  • Interactive fixed effects (Bai 2009) estimate the factors and loadings by principal components instead of proxying them. They need the number of factors chosen in advance and more of T; CCE needs neither, which is why this book uses CCE first.

B.6 5. If / then

If Then
α ≈ 1, or CD rejects on the raw series Year effects are not enough. CCE (Pesaran 2006).
CCEMG and CCEP agree within a standard error One common slope. Report CCEP, keep CCEMG as the check (Croissant and Millo 2019, 199).
CCEMG and CCEP differ by more than that Heterogeneous slopes. Report CCEMG; its variance is the dispersion of the slopes.
α on the CCE residuals is still near 1 A factor was missed: more factors than averages. State it as a limitation.
The series wander CCE still holds (Kapetanios et al. 2011). Test the residuals for a unit root (tests that allow a common factor).
A lagged outcome is a regressor Short-T bias. Lags of the averages (Chudik and Pesaran 2015), not plain CCE.

B.7 6. Open

  • The number of factors is never tested here. The rank condition is assumed, and the only check is the exponent on the residuals, which can say that something was missed but not what.
  • Rank-condition failure. The conditions under which CCE survives it are in Pesaran (2006) and are not verified for the house-price panel.
  • Heterogeneous slopes in CCEP. The sandwich variance assumes the slope deviations are random and independent of the regressors; the step does not test it.

B.8 Next

Panel unit roots takes the CCE residuals to the next question, whether price and income share one trend. Testing for spatial dependence takes the other branch, where the averages carry nothing and the map carries everything.
